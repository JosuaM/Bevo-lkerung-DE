Summary 
Do reusable plastic crates perform better compared to traditional baskets? 
The study highlights the challenges of introducing reusable plastic crates as an alternative packaging 
solution in South Benin’s tomato value chain. The study produced mixed outcomes, making it difficult 
to state that reusable plastic crates perform better compared to traditional baskets.  
 
Reusable plastic crates show potential for reducing post-harvest losses and increasing value in 
the tomato value chain, but their adoption faces challenges in cost and logistics, necessitating 
innovative business models for scalability. 
The results showed variability in post-harvest losses across packaging methods. While reusable plastic 
crates demonstrated slightly lower post-harvest losses compared to traditional baskets, the 
differences were not substantial. Loss percentages from farm to the retail level were inconsistent, with 
in some cases no clear trend favoring crates over traditional methods. 
Our study indicates a small opportunity to increase value across all stakeholders in the value chain by 
adopting improved packaging solutions. In the first round of assessments, four value chains 
demonstrated significantly higher monetary losses when using traditional baskets compared to 
reusable plastic crates. While crates preserved quality slightly better, the marginal improvement in 
prices did not offset the additional costs associated with the investment. Participants noted that the 
initial investment in crates posed a significant barrier, especially given the limited observed advantages 
during the pilot.  
Feedback highlighted logistical challenges with crate availability and the need for better integration 
into existing workflows. Some participants had concerns about limited tomato availability during the 
study period and this further complicated the data collection, particularly during the second round of 
data collection. 
Developing a sustainable business model for the returnable plastic crate intervention poses 
challenges. Implementing requires substantial investments, which are beyond the financial capacity of 
the participants. As a result, exploring alternative funding mechanisms or business models is essential 
to ensure the project's feasibility and long-term success.

initiative. Ouidah has a large agricultural center where many different fruits and vegetables are

1. Introduction 
Good postharvest management of vegetables is a critical challenge for value chain actors, as these 
crops exhibit significantly higher post-harvest losses compared to cereals or pulses. In Benin, 
tomatoes, characterized by a high-water content (85%-95%) (Aisso, et al., 2018), are particularly prone 
to spoilage due to inadequate packaging and poor handling during transportation and storage 
(Fassinou Hotegni et al. 2022). Traditional methods such as using traditional baskets lead to significant 
losses, estimated at 6.9% to 16.5% of the total volume from farm to wholesale levels (Kablan and 
Bancal, 2019). Additionally, damaged tomatoes are sold at reduced prices, further exacerbating 
economic losses for farmers and retailers. As a result, the post-harvest losses also contribute to 
economic losses for the farmers and the wholesalers. Using other packaging units such as reusable 
plastic crates for transportation and storage could offer a good alternative to reduce the post-harvest 
losses of tomatoes (Kok et al., 2019).  
 
The adoption of innovative packaging solutions in Nigeria has significantly reduced post-harvest losses 
and improved product quality for perishable goods, including tomatoes (Plaisier et al., 2019; Kok et al 
2019). These materials have proven particularly effective in maintaining the quality of tomatoes during 
transportation and storage, enhancing their market value. Given the similarities in agricultural 
practices and market conditions, introducing such packing materials in Benin presents a viable 
opportunity to address storage and transportation challenges.  
 
This study assesses the effectiveness of reusable plastic crates as an alternative packaging solution to 
reduce post-harvest losses and increase economic value in South Benin’s tomato value chain. In this 
report we will answer the following research question: 
 
 Do reusable plastic crates perform better compared to traditional baskets? 
 
Sub-research questions: 
 Does the use of reusable plastic crates reduce tomato post-harvest losses compared to the use 
of traditional baskets? 
 Does the use of reusable plastic crates increase the value of the tomatoes traded in the value 
chain compared to traditional baskets? 
 How are the plastic crates perceived by the stakeholders in the tomato value chain and what 
are the opportunities for scaling?  
 What is a feasible business model for the implementation of this innovation?  
 
Piloting this approach strengths the value chain by reducing losses, improving the value in the entire 
value chain of fresh produce, and aligning with the broader goals of the CGIAR initiative on Fruit and 
Vegetables for Sustainable Healthy Diets (FRESH).

2. Methodology 
2.1 Study area 
The study was conducted in Ouidah in Benin. Farmers in Ouidah receive support from the FRESH 
initiative. Ouidah has a large agricultural center where many different fruits and vegetables are 
produced and traded in large volumes. The FRESH hub is accessible by a main road that has recently 
been built. The main fruit and vegetable crops cultivated are tomato, gboma, chilli, watermelon, 
vernonia, African basilic (tchiayo), amaranth, cucumber, cabbage, and onion (Egah et al., 2024). This 
study describes the trade dynamics in the area and explains, amongst others, that the unit of 
measurement of these products varies. Tomatoes are sold by stakeholders using a basket, whereas 
fruits such as watermelons, gherkins, cucumbers, and melons are sold per unit according to size of the 
fruit after harvesting (the larger, the higher the price). Leafy vegetables are sold by land portion (about 
5 m² of area) on the farm to wholesalers. Fruit and vegetables are sold on inside and outside markets. 
This system of selling per basket is not kg-based and this easily allows for overlading of the units, 
affecting the quality of the produce and resulting in post-harvest losses (PHL).  
 
The local markets are close to the production site. More distant markets are in other communes, 
specifically in larger towns such as Tokpa in Cotonou, Togba and Akassato in Abomey-Calavi. Distant 
markets are targeted when farmers cannot find local buyers during periods of abundance. During 
periods of abundance, the sale price is lower, as farmers accept the price fixed by the traders on these 
markets. In periods of scarcity, wholesalers visit the farmers and they define sales agreements on the 
spot. 
 
2.2. Participants and sampling approach 
The study targeted midstream actors, including wholesalers, retailers, transporters, and farmers active 
in the tomato value chain. Five wholesalers were selected as primary participants using a snowball 
sampling technique. These wholesalers facilitated the identification of other midstream actors in the 
same value chain. Consent and availability to participate in all study activities were obtained from 
participants, including stakeholder workshops. 
 
2.3 Study design and implementation 
The study followed a participatory and comparative design, involving three key steps (preparation 
phase, implementation phase and final phase). 
 Preparation phase: The preparation phase focused on mobilizing stakeholders from value 
chains of tomato through workshops and exploratory interviews, making the reusable 
plastic crates and measurement tools available, and developing questionnaires to collect 
data on socioeconomic indicators, weight and quality of tomatoes, and perceptions of 
packaging methods. Enumerators were trained to ensure accurate data collection and 
adherence to the study’s protocol. The questionnaire and study protocol were based on a 
previously used structure as used in other PHL measurement projects implemented by WUR 
(see for example Kok et al., 2019). 
 Implementation phase: During the implementation phase from the mid of October to the 
mid of November, tomatoes were packed in reusable plastic crates, weighed baskets and

not filled above the top of the basket, and traditionally packed baskets without weighing 
(baseline). See picture 1A below with the different packing types. The weighing was added as

 Final phase: The final phase involved data analysis and reporting. Descriptive statistics were 
used, and postharvest losses were calculated by comparing initial and final weights per 
value chain. Economic feasibility and social perceptions were analyzed, and findings were 
shared through a final feedback workshop with all involved participants (December 2024) to 
capitalize on lessons learned and share the results of the pilot with the participants.

3.3 Findings from feedback workshop and opportunities for scaling 
Adoption barriers 
Stakeholder feedback revealed several logistical challenges associated with the use of reusable plastic 
crates. These included issues with crate availability and the difficulty of integrating their use into 
existing workflows. Some participants questioned the feasibility of adopting plastic crates under 
current market conditions, particularly given concerns about limited tomato availability during the 
study period. Additionally, the proximity of farms to markets reduced the potential benefits of 
improved packaging, as shorter transport durations minimized spoilage risks.  
 
The study’s findings were further constrained by a limited sample size and seasonal challenges. 
Stakeholders raised concerns about the scalability of reusable plastic crates, particularly in the 
absence of financial incentives such as government subsidies. They emphasized the need for further 
testing under varying conditions and distances to better assess the potential benefits of crate 
adoption. See Table 1 for an overview of the observations on suggested recommendations.

Stakeholder perception of reusable plastic crates   
The discussion during the workshop highlighted both the advantages and limitations of reusable 
plastic crate use compared to traditional baskets.   
 
Advantages mentioned:    
 Elimination of the need for wood supports during loading.   
 Reduced effort required for tying plastic crates.   
 Easier stacking compared to baskets.   
 Protection of tomatoes from direct damage by ropes. 
 
Limitations mentioned:  
 Resistance from traders to accept equivalent quantities between crates and baskets.   
 Challenges in transporting larger quantities of crates, particularly for smaller vehicles like 
tricycles. 
 
17 
 
Scaling up reusable plastic crate adoption  
Stakeholders emphasized the importance of engaging a wide range of actors to scale up the use of 
reusable plastic crates (awareness raising). Key players mentioned include:   
 Local extension services.   
 Regional agricultural development agencies (ATDA).   
 Partner projects such as SafeVeg, Support Project for the Development of Market Gardening 
(PADMAR), and AgriSud.   
 National and local government bodies, including the ministries of agriculture and trade.   
 Private investors to rent-out crates to value chain actors, as a business.   
 National Federation of gardener Organizations of Benin (FeNOMA) to coordinate the 
standardization of unit sizes at the national level. 
They also mentioned to consider establishing monitoring committees at the district level to oversee 
crate usage and discourage reverting to traditional baskets.   
 
3.4 Feasibility assessment  
The business model for reusable plastic crates in Benin faces significant challenges in availability, cost, 
and leadership for implementation. Currently, there is no local production of reusable plastic crates 
due to a lack of demand, which has deterred manufacturers of plastic materials from entering this 
market. As a result, crates must be imported from Ghana or Nigeria, driving up costs to a minimum of 
6,000 CFA (or USD10) per crate, making them prohibitively expensive for value chain actors to 
purchase. This relatively high cost poses a major barrier for adoption, particularly in a context where 
access to finance is limited. Leadership in implementing this model remains unclear. However, 
experiences from other contexts, such as Nigeria, suggest that external investors—often from outside 
the tomato value chain —can play a pivotal role by introducing crate rental services, reducing the 
upfront costs for local actors. As highlighted in the forthcoming study by Becker et al., such rental 
services provide a viable pathway to overcome the financial and logistical barriers associated with 
direct crate ownership, offering a potential solution for the Benin context. In Nigeria, the widespread 
adoption of reusable plastic crates was driven by several factors: the identification of a profitable 
business opportunity by crate renters, the complementary benefits of reduced postharvest losses, and 
the immediate, positive return on investment experienced by users across the value chain. The 
initiative covered the North, Nigeria's tomato production hub, where significant seasonal losses 
necessitated innovative solutions for transporting tomatoes to southern markets (Becker et al., 
forthcoming). 
 
In Nigeria, the emergence of crate renters as new entrants into the value chain was pivotal. These 
investors, many of whom were already active in tomato trading, recognized the growing demand for 
reusable plastic crates and formed an association to coordinate their activities. The association 
ensured exclusivity by negotiating MOUs with major plastic manufacturers to create unique crate 
designs and colour codes reserved for their members. This not only protected their business but also 
facilitated the identification and return of crates. 
 
18 
 
The association established an efficient system for returning crates from southern markets to the 
North. Coordinators at southern markets, well-connected with market leadership, managed crate 
collections and arranged transport for large volumes (minimum 1,500 crates) back to aggregation 
centers in the North. Crate renters recovered their investments after just seven uses per crate, with 
crates used up to four times per month, translating into profitability within two months. In Nigeria the 
crates had a lifespan of up to two years and beyond, making the business lucrative despite risks such 
as loss, damage, or delays. 
 
In Benin, the lack of local manufacturing and high costs per crate make it difficult for individual value 
chain actors to adopt reusable plastic crates. However, replicating the Nigerian model, where investors 
from outside the sector lead the introduction of crate rental services, could be transformative. 
Establishing a crate renters' business in Benin could address operational challenges and ensure 
efficient collection, return, and redistribution of crates. Additionally, highlighting the reduction of 
postharvest losses and potential profitability could attract investors, while strategic partnerships with 
manufacturers or importers could ensure a steady supply of crates with unique designs.  
 
4. Conclusion and discussion  
4.1 Conclusion  
The study highlights the challenges of introducing reusable plastic crates as an alternative packaging 
solution in South Benin’s tomato value chain. The study produced mixed outcomes, making it difficult 
to state that reusable plastic crates perform better compared to traditional baskets. The study also 
revealed limitations of the intervention due to contextual factors.  
 Does the use of reusable plastic crates reduce tomato post-harvest losses compared to the use of 
traditional baskets? 
The results showed variability in post-harvest losses across packaging methods. While reusable plastic 
crates demonstrated slightly lower post-harvest losses compared to traditional baskets (in round 1), 
the differences were not significant. Loss percentages from farm to retail level were inconsistent, with 
in some cases no clear trend favoring crates over traditional methods. The distance to the market 
might have influenced this and the proximity of farms to markets reduced the potential impact of 
improved packaging, as shorter transport durations minimized spoilage risks. The limited sample size, 
the differences in packing, the difference in weight between the units and seasonal constraints also 
limit the robustness of the findings.  
 Does the use of reusable plastic increase the value of the tomatoes traded in the value chain 
compared to traditional baskets? 
Our study indicates a small opportunity to increase value across all stakeholders in the value chain by 
adopting improved packaging solutions. In the first round of assessments, four value chains 
demonstrated significantly higher monetary losses when using traditional baskets compared to 
reusable plastic crates. Data collection in the second round was affected by poor weather conditions 
and showed contradicting results.

Feedback highlighted logistical challenges with crate availability and the need for better integration 
into existing workflows. Some participants had concerns about limited tomato availability during the 
study period and this further complicated the data collection, particularly during the second round of 
data collection. 
 What is a feasible business model for the implementation of this innovation?  
Developing a sustainable business model for the returnable plastic crate intervention poses 
challenges. Implementing requires substantial investments, which are beyond the financial capacity of 
the participants. As a result, exploring alternative funding mechanisms or business models is essential 
to ensure the project's feasibility and long-term success. 
 
4.2 Discussion 
The study revealed two distinct categories of issues that we address in the discussion: limitations 
related to the study design and challenges associated with the practical uptake of the proposed 
innovations. 
 
Limitations of the study design 
The small sample size was a significant limitation, restricting the generalizability of the findings. 
Additionally, the proximity of the farmers' markets to the farms minimized transit times, reducing the 
potential for fruit deterioration during transport. As a result, the outcomes were influenced by the 
initial quality of the produce at the farm level rather than the impact of the interventions during 
transport and handling.  
 
Variability in the quantities supplied also posed a challenge, as some farmers used smaller baskets, 
and the crates were inconsistently filled, further complicating efforts to achieve uniformity in 
monitoring the effects of the various practices. 
 
It would be valuable to repeat the pilot during both the peak supply period and over a longer distance 
to identify benefits. Also when tomatoes are more expensive, the potential economic benefits of 
packing them in reusable plastic crates are likely to be more pronounced. 
 
Challenges regarding uptake 
The study also highlighted several barriers to the practical adoption of the innovations. One critical 
issue was the lack of local reusable plastic crate manufacturers, which made crates prohibitively 
expensive for many farmers. Additionally, the size of the crates required adjustment to better align 
with user needs and transport requirements. Participants noted that the high initial investment 
needed for crates posed a significant financial barrier, particularly in the absence of clear short-term 
20 
 
advantages. These economic constraints underscored the need for addressing socioeconomic barriers 
to adoption. 
 
To encourage broader uptake, future efforts should focus on exploring ways to make innovations like 
reusable plastic crates more accessible and affordable while also testing under a wider range of 
conditions. Socioeconomic factors, such as the cost-benefit perception among farmers and midstream 
actors, must be considered alongside strategies to enhance awareness, improve affordability (e.g. 
financial incentives from the government like subsidies) and address logistical challenges and 
ownership issues. These steps are essential to ensure the long-term success of post-harvest loss 
reduction initiatives.
