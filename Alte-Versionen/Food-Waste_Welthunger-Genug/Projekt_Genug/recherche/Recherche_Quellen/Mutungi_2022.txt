Nutritional benefits of improved post-harvest handling practices for maize 
and common beans in Northern Tanzania: A quantitative 
farm-level assessment

International Institute of Tropical Agriculture, Dar es Salaam, Tanzania

ABSTRACT  
The efficacy of improved post-harvest practices for reducing food losses is often reported, but the nutritional 
benefits remain much less expounded. Meanwhile, nutrient concentration changes during the post-harvest period 
can influence potential nutrition gains targeted to benefit households. We analyzed contents of selected nutrients 
in farmers ’ maize and common bean stocks under ordinary and improved practices at different post-harvest 
stages and related them to the physical quality. The improved practices comprised drying on tarpaulins, 
improved threshing, cleaning, and air-tight storage. By calibrating the actual nutrient contents against losses 
data, we estimated nutrient abundance levels and applied household farm production and nutrient demand data 
to estimate potential nutritional gains. Post-harvest stage (S), practice (P), and the S*P interaction significantly 
influenced physical quality, actual nutrient contents, and nutrient abundance. The actual contents of most nu-
trients increased or decreased monotonically with grain damage. For maize, improved practices begot more 
calories (4.7%), protein (13.9%), and minerals (4 – 26%) at the mid stage of the post-harvest season and even 
more calories (40%), protein (34%), and minerals (2 – 105%) at the late stage. The gains were lower for 
beans– calories 6.5%, protein 8.7%, minerals 1 – 55%. Dry matter loss and nutrient enrichment effects were 
responsible for variable nutrient concentrations in the maize and beans. The dry matter loss effect mainly 
influenced macronutrient abundance levels, especially in maize, whereas the enrichment effect mainly influ-
enced minerals abundance with stronger influence on the beans than maize. Nutrients redeemed by improved 
practices at the late stage corresponded to 16 – 55 more days of calories and protein sufficiency for households, 
and significant extra days of minerals sufficiency, including Zn (16– 56), Cu (23– 81), Mg (45– 166), and K (7– 26). 
The gains have relevance for nutritionally-challenged households.   
1. Introduction 
Across East and Southern Africa, maize ( Zea mays L.) and common 
beans ( Phaseolus vulgaris L.) contribute at least 40% of the calories 
consumed daily by households (
FAOSTAT, 2018). The two crops are 
among the most widely cultivated commodities in small-holder farms, 
usually as intercrops or mixed-stands. By cultivating these crops 
together, farm households achieve more efficient use of production re-
sources, minimize crop failure risks and obtain a balanced nutrition. 
However, the overall food production in the small farms is often low, to 
the extent that post-production losses effectively drive households out of 
food and nutrition security. To this end, reducing food loss is a crucial 
part of sustainable food production (
Godfray et al., 2010). Indeed, food 
loss/waste reduction is one of the elements for consideration in char-
acterizing sustainable nutrition outcomes of food systems (
Gustafson 
et al., 2016 ) and is an integral indicator for measuring productivity 
within the frame of sustainable agriculture intensification ( Musumba 
et al., 2017). Post-harvest food loss connotes the measurable reduction 
in quantity or quality of harvested produce, and is driven mainly by the 
lack of appropriate technologies for harvesting, processing, storage, 
distribution, marketing, or preparation (
Affognon et al., 2015). 
In Tanzania, the production of maize and beans takes about 30% 
(maize) and 9% (beans) of the cultivated land area (FAOSTAT, 2018). At 
least 7.4 million farm producers grow maize, and the national per capita 
consumption is estimated at 63 kg (
FAOSTAT, 2018 ), translating to 
approximately 25% and 26% of the adult recommended daily allowance

The two commodities complement each other uniquely for nutritional 
balance. Beans primarily contribute protein, minerals, and B-complex 
vitamins, whereas maize is principally a calorie source but also provides 
significant quantities of protein and minerals due to the large amounts 
consumed in diets. In addition, bean protein contains fair amounts of the 
amino acids lysine and tryptophan deficient in maize, while it lacks 
sulfur amino acids (methionine and cysteine) present in maize protein. 
As reported elsewhere (
Broughton et al., 2003), maize and beans com-
plement each other for the recommended amino acid balance if 
consumed in a ratio of 2:1. 
There are numerous publications on maize and beans ’ quantitative 
losses and the associated economic costs (
Affognon et al., 2015 ). 
Nutritionally, however, the implications are less understood. In Tanza-
nia’s maize-farming systems, quantitative losses can reach 40%. The 
losses of economic importance occur in the field (15%), during pro-
cessing (13 – 20%), and during storage (15 – 25%) (
Abass et al., 2014 ). 
About 2 – 13% of the losses in maize are due to improper harvesting, 
threshing, and drying techniques ( Abass et al., 2014 ; Mutungi et al., 
2019). At the same time, up to 5% losses occur during the operations 
preceding storage of beans and 10– 40% during storage (Stathers et al., 
2008; Mutungi et al., 2020). The physical damage associated with these 
losses can exceed 70%. Such damage and losses are quantified mostly on 
a weight basis relative to a considered baseline. As a result, the nutri-
tional implications become misrepresented because commodities differ 
in their specific nutrient compositions, and the concentration of some 
nutrients may continually change during the post-harvest period. 
The ability of food systems to provide sufficient energy and the 
amounts of essential nutrients required for healthy nutrition without 
upsetting the social, economic, and environmental balances is critical 
for sustainability. Some works already define metrics that quantify 
nutrient production at the farm level (e.g., calories, protein, micro-
nutrients quantities per hectare) as proxies for nutrition as they provide 
direct information on ’’ potential nutrients sufficiency ’’ of households 
(
Musumba et al., 2017; Smith et al., 2017). For correction, such metrics 
require an understanding of the post-production nutrient flows and 
balances. In the context of post-harvest food loss, incomplete collection 
of the harvest, spillage, and extraction of parts during processing 
translates into a complete loss of the nutrients. Likewise, consumer 
rejection of products whose quality fails the acceptance thresholds 
represents nutrients loss. Adding to the complexity of quantifying the 
nutrient losses is the fact that individual consumers assess quality 
differently depending on a myriad of factors, including the commodity 
in question, supply, personal aversion for different kinds of quality 
damage, et cetera (
Compton et al., 1998; Langyintuo et al., 2004; Mishili 
et al., 2011; Kadjo et al., 2016; Bassey et al., 2018; Jones et al., 2018). 
Moreover, the various food deterioration factors (growth and activities 
of micro-organisms – bacteria, yeasts, and molds; activities of natural 
food enzymes; attack by insects and rodent pests; extreme temperatures; 
excessive moisture and dryness; gas composition, in particular, oxygen; 
and light) also contribute varying levels of nutritional value changes. 
Edoh-Ognakossan et al. (2018) found lower levels of protein 
(13.3%), fat (87.9%), and fatty acids (58 – 85%) in rodent-damaged 
maize, while Reed et al. (2007) reported fat and carbohydrate loss but 
protein gain in moldy maize. Insect-infested maize and cowpeas con-
tained lower carbohydrate, iron, and zinc but higher crude protein 
(
Stathers et al., 2020 ). Higher protein contents were also reported in 
insect-damaged wheat ( Jood and Kapoor, 1993 ; Keskin and Ozkaya, 
2015) and sorghum (Jood and Kapoor, 1993) as well as maize (Jood and 
Kapoor, 1993; Tongjura et al., 2010), and cowpea (Bamaiyi et al., 2006). 
Other researchers, on the contrary, reported decreases in protein con-
tent, e.g., in sorghum (
Pant and Susheela, 1977 ), cowpea ( Onayemi 
et al., 1986; Ojimelukwe et al., 1999 ), and common beans ( Nta et al., 
2019). Increases in the fat content of cowpea (Stathers et al., 2020) and 
beans (Nta et al., 2019 ), but decreases on wheat ( Keskin and Ozkaya, 
2015), sorghum (Pant and Susheela, 1977), and also cowpea (Onayemi 
et al., 1986 ; Ojimelukwe et al., 1999 ; Bamaiyi et al., 2006 ) were re-
ported. Variable changes in the concentration of minerals and vitamins 
(
Jood et al., 1992; Jood and Kapoor, 1994; Taleon et al., 2017; Stathers 
et al., 2020), as well as amino acids, were likewise observed (Jood et al., 
1995; Rehman et al., 2002). 
To the best of our knowledge, systematic investigations linking these 
nutritional changes to the potential implications for farm households are 
limited. A recent controlled laboratory study on the effect of insect 
infestation concluded that significant nutritional changes do occur on 
maize and cowpeas, and recommended further on-farm investigations to 
validate the findings (
Stathers et al., 2020 ). No matter how carefully 
collected, data under laboratory conditions have limited scope and use 
in contextualizing impacts at the farm level where variables (biophysi-
cal, social, economic, human) are transient and influence each other. For 
instance, some farmers harvest early because they urgently need to 
secure the food or the crop residues as fodder for their livestock, create 
space for intercrops or make room for the next crop. The early harvest is 
sometimes dried hurriedly, often directly on the ground, and then 
threshed, at times using rudimentary methods such as hand pickets or 
poorly calibrated threshers (
Mutungi et al., 2019). Other farmers harvest 
late for various reasons, such as lack of labor or equipment. Earlier 
findings showed that delayed harvesting of maize increased moldy/di-
seased/discolored grain two-fold, while drying operations of the 
early-harvested produce increased these defects 2– 3-fold and increased 
pre-storage insect populations 2– 10-fold, affecting storability (
Mutungi 
et al., 2019). In recent years, improved post-harvest techniques– drying 
on enhanced tarpaulins, motorized threshing, and air-tight storage were 
promoted to help farmers reduce quality deterioration and quantity 
losses more inclusively. 
This study aimed to quantify the nutritional gains of the improved 
post-harvest practices over ordinary practices by empirically correlating 
the physical quality and nutrient contents of farmers ’ maize and com-
mon bean stocks in small-holder settings. A further aim was to generate 
models for estimating such gains. We make a new contribution to the 
literature by employing a back-to-back analysis of on-farm samples ’ 
quality and nutrient contents to understand nutritional content shifts, 
which we then relate with quantitative losses, and households ’ pro-
duction and nutrients demand data to provide a contextual interpreta-
tion. We framed the assessment on two interest levels: nutrient 
composition and nutrient abundance. The hypotheses were that (a) at 
the compositional level, grain handled under ordinary practices contain 
significantly lower nutrient amounts due to deterioration, and (b) the 
overall abundance of nutrients is significantly diminished because nu-
trients become extracted from the supply chain following damage by 
various post-harvest loss agents. Restricting the scope to farmers’ stocks, 
we evaluated the associations between selected physical quality pa-
rameters and the macronutrients/mineral concentrations of maize and 
common beans at the time of loading for storage (loading stage) and the 
mid and late stages of the post-harvest period. We asked the following 
research questions: (i) What types and levels of physical defects 
constitute losses in farmers’ maize and common bean stocks at different 
post-harvest stages under ordinary and improved practices? (ii) Are 
there significant changes in the macronutrient and minerals contents of 
the stocks at the different post-harvest stages? (iii) Are there relation-
ships between physical quality and nutrient contents? (iv) What are the 
potential nutritional gains of improved post-harvest practices for 
households? Answering these questions would provide nutritionally- 
grounded evidence for engaging farm communities on the value of 
progressive food loss reduction investments.

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Study site background information 
Demonstration trials on improved post-harvest handling and storage 
of maize and beans were installed with farmer groups in eight (8) rural 
villages (Buger, Chemchem, Kambi ya Simba, Khainam Rhotia, Chan-
garawe, Slahhamo, Gyekrum Lambo, and Bashay) in Karatu district 
(Arusha region) during the 2018/2019 crop season. The farmers were an 
intervention cohort supported by a non-governmental organization (Iles 
de Paix) whose activities focused on sustainable family farming. Karatu 
district lies in the Northern Highlands of Tanzania at 1200– 1800 m.a.s.l 
and experiences a warm-temperate climate with a bimodal rainfall 
pattern (
Fig. 1). The wet season begins in November and ends in May, 
giving way to a dry season from June to October. Short- and long-rain 
seasons peak in November/December and March/April, respectively. 
The annual temperature average is ~20 
◦C; warmer conditions prevail in 
the wet season, while cooler conditions prevail during the dry season. 
The post-harvest season commences around July/August and continues 
to April/May, lasting 8– 9 months. Some households, particularly those 
in high-altitude areas, cultivate short-season crops during the short rains 
to bridge food deficits between the main harvests, but generally, grain 
stocks in most households decline significantly from February and a lean 
period continues for at least 2 – 3 months. The majority of farmers are 
small-holders, practicing mixed– crop farming comprising maize (100% 
of households), pigeon peas (74%), beans (71%), and sunflower (33%) 
fruits and vegetables (13%) on 2 – 3 acres of land. On average, house-
holds cultivate 2 – 3 different crops and keep livestock on a semi- 
intensive scale (see 
Fig. 1). About 75% of households keep cattle (me-
dian number (M) = 3), 60% goats (M = 4), 34% sheep (M = 4), 83% 
chicken (M = 8), and 36% pigs (M = 2). Maize and beans are the main 
crops cultivated for domestic consumption. At the time of the study, 
pigeon peas were cultivated mainly for sale, while sunflower was grown 
in small quantities to supply oil for domestic consumption. 
From past findings, post-harvest practices vary widely in Tanzania’s 
Northern Highlands (
Mutungi et al., 2019). Maize is harvested early by 
about two– thirds of farmers. Most farmers in the present study site dried 
the harvest (cob and pods) on the bare ground, and only a minority used 
tarpaulins. Crops took a long time to dry because the harvest season 
coincided with cold weather. Other farmers left the mature crop for

several weeks in the field to dry, after which they harvested, threshed, 
and sold right away to waiting buyers in the field or transported to the 
homestead for storage. About a quarter (24%) of farmers did not dry the 
maize further after shelling. Before shelling, farmers sorted the cobs to 
different extents; some sorted based on mold damage (71.7%), rot 
(46.2%), insect damage (21%), rodent damage (12.3%), maturity 
(11.2%), color (2.8%), size (2.8%), and sprout damage (1.9%). Most 
farmers used motorized maize shellers available from service providers, 
but manual methods were common for small harvests. Farmers har-
vested beans early only when maturity coincided with wet weather and 
transported them to the homestead for drying on raised platforms 
(Kichanja); otherwise, they left them to dry in the field, then gathered 
them manually into heaps for threshing using hand pickets. Farmers 
winnowed the threshed produce manually, but some stored together 
with the fine chaff mostly in woven bags, drums, or traditional 
mud-plastered baskets (Kihenge). 
2.2. Experimental details 
A baseline survey (n = 166) provided households ’ production and 
demographic data of farmers in the action villages. Two farmer groups in 
each village received training on a set of improved practices three 
months before the harvest season. The practices comprised timely har-
vesting, drying the cobs on mats, improved threshing practices, drying 
the threshed grain on mats, moisture testing, winnowing/sieving, and 
storage in air-tight containers. Each group then appointed 3 – 4 farmers 
to set up the trials. These were identified based on community trust and 
willingness to volunteer storage structures throughout the post-harvest 
season in their homesteads. Each farmer prepared and stored part of 
their maize and beans produce according to the improved practices 
under the guidance of a field staff and part according to the usual/or-
dinary practices. There were 111 demonstration units for maize 
(improved: 77; ordinary: 34) and 24 for common beans (improved: 12, 
ordinary: 12). The storage structures volunteered by farmers were rooms 
(separate from their usual grain stores) made of brick or mud walls, 
earthen or concrete floor, and iron-sheet roofing. Extension officers and 
field research staff monitored the trials regularly. 
2.3. Sampling 
Twenty-four (24) out of the 111 maize demonstration units 
(improved: 12; ordinary: 12) were randomly chosen for repeated sam-
pling, in addition to all the 24 units (improved: 12; ordinary: 12) 
involving common beans. Samples (1 kg) were obtained at the time of 
loading, after 3.5 months (mid stage), and 7 months (late stage), and 
transported to IITA’s Food Quality Laboratory (Dar es Salaam, Tanzania) 
within 3 – 5 days of sampling. Each sample was divided into two sub- 
samples (ca. 500 g) using the quartering method. One random sub- 
sample was marked for physical quality assessment, and the other for 
nutritional analysis. The sub-samples were sealed in zip-lock bags and 
stored in a cold room (6 – 8 ◦C), awaiting analysis within 14 days. The 
analysis generated paired data of physical quality and nutritional 
content. 
2.4. Determination of physical quality and losses 
Insect damaged grains (ID), moldy/diseased/discolored grains 
(MDD), mechanically damaged grains (MD), shriveled grains (SG), total 
defective grains (TD), and losses (WL) were assessed according to the 
method described elsewhere (
Mutungi et al., 2019). Briefly, the method 
applies a “ count and weigh” approach in which 1000 random grains are 
picked and manually sorted according to the dominant defect on a 
sorting board. Further, using technicians conversant with local con-
sumers’ tolerance limits, the defective grains are separated into 
consumable and non-consumable lots, imitating consumers ’ accept-
ance/rejection criteria as closely as possible. 
2.5. Determination of nutrient contents 
Proximate composition and mineral contents were determined using 
standard AOAC methods (
AOAC, 2000). Entire sub-samples (ca. 500 g) 
were separately milled into flour using a DFY-400C universal mill 
(Toption Instrument Co. Ltd, Xi ’an, China). Moisture content was 
determined by hot-air drying (Method 925.10). Crude protein was 
determined using the Kjeldahl method for total nitrogen with a con-
version factor of 6.25 (Method 920.87) — samples were digested in a 
Tecator™ Digester (FOSS Analytical AB, H¨ogan¨as, Sweden) and distilled 
using a Kjeltic™ 8200 auto distillation unit (Foss Analytical AB). Crude 
fat was determined by soxhlet extraction (Method 920.85) on a FOSS 
Soxtec™ 2043 unit (Foss Analytical AB). The crude fiber was deter-
mined using hot acid (1.25% hydrochloric acid) followed by hot alkaline 
(1.25% potassium hydroxide) digestion on Fibertec ™ 1020 
hot-extraction unit (Foss Analytical AB). Crude ash (total minerals) 
determination was by incineration in an MR170 muffle furnace (Heraeus 
GMBH, Hanau, Germany) at 550 
◦C (Method 923.03). Total carbohy-
drate content was obtained by difference. Mineral contents (Ca, Zn, Fe, 
Mg, K, Mn, Cu, and Na) were determined using flame atomic absorption 
spectrophotometry (210 VGP Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer, 
Buck Scientific, Norwalk, CT, USA) as described elsewhere (
Rybicka and 
Gliszczy´nska-´Swigło, 2017 ). All determinations were performed in 
triplicates. 
2.6. Statistical analysis 
The physical quality and losses data were expressed as percentages 
on a weight basis. The nutritional data were expressed as (i) actual 
nutrient contents (ANC) per unit weight of the analyzed sample, and (ii) 
loss-adjusted nutrient abundance (LANA) — derived by multiplying the 
ANC with a corresponding loss factor computed from the losses data as 
(1 - x) where x is percent loss expressed as a fraction. Further, the 
calorific values were calculated using standard formulae: maize kcal/ 
100 g = (4.03 × carbohydrate g/100 g) + (8.37 × fat g/100 g) + (2.73 
× protein g/100 g); beans: kcal/100 g = (4.07 × carbohydrate g/100 g) 
+ (8.37 × fat g/100 g) + (3.47 × protein g/100 g) (
FAO, 2003). 
Data were analyzed using multivariate General Linear Models on 
IBM SPSS ® Statistics 20 (IBM Corporation, New York, USA). Three 
separate multivariate analyses of variance (MANOVA) tests explored the 
main and interaction effects of practice and post-harvest stage on (i) 
physical quality, (ii) ANC, and (iii) LANA. For physical quality, we 
considered an experimental matrix consisting of the two independent 
variables and their levels (practice: improved, ordinary; post-harvest 
stage: loading, mid, late), and the six dependent variables (ID, MDD, 
MD, SG, TD, and WL) grouped together. For the ANC and LANA, we 
considered experimental matrices consisting of the two independent 
variables and their levels and 13 dependent variables (protein, fat, fiber, 
carbohydrate, ash, Ca, Zn, Fe, Mg, K, Mn, Cu, and Na) grouped together. 
For the analyses, we first checked the data sets for the critical assump-
tions of MANOVA, i.e., normality, multivariate outliers, multi-
collinearity, variables correlation, and homogeneity of covariance 
matrices. The data were not normally distributed (Shapiro-Wilk’s test P 
< 0.05). Therefore, when required, non-parametric methods (Kruskal- 
Wallis test, Spearman’s rho) were applied to compare treatment means 
or variables ’ correlations. Multivariate outliers were checked by per-
forming a multiple linear regression and computing the Mahalanobis 
distance for each observation case; these values were then compared 
with the critical chi-square values at P = 0.001, and the outlier cases 
were eliminated. Multicollinearity was tested by performing collinearity 
diagnostics and evaluating the Variance Inflation Factors (VIF). The 
assumption for absence of multicollinearity was considered to be satis-
fied if the VIF values for all variables remained <4 (
Hair et al., 2014). 
The homogeneity of covariance matrices test was part of the MANOVA 
using Box ’s M test. The data did not meet this last assumption ( P <
0.001); hence Pillai’s Trace statistic was used for the reporting as it is

3. Results 
3.1. Physical quality of maize and beans 
3.1.1. Physical quality damage 
Farmers’ maize and beans stocks had various physical defects 
(
Fig. 2). The MD included broken or visibly bruised grains from the 
impact of threshing or winnowing and grains gouged by rodents or other 
pests. The ID comprised grains with visible weevil or bruchid attacks. 
The SG included poorly-filled wrinkled grains, whereas MDD comprised 
grains with visible mold damage or discoloration due to biochemical 
reactions, overheating, or disease/decay from biological agents (en-
zymes, bacteria, yeasts). Insect damage was the predominant defect, 
whereas the other defects— MDD, MD, and SG were low but significant 
when considered collectively; these ranged between 4.2 – 24% (maize) 
and 2– 13.8% (beans). Overall, both commodities ’ range and distribu-
tions of total damage (TD) were similar. The distributions exhibited 
bimodal patterns (
Fig. 3a) with density peaks at ~10% and ~80% under 
ordinary practices and ~5% and ~10% under improved practices. The 
TD inter-quartile range reached 4.4 – 11.6% (maize) and 3.6 – 11.7% 
(beans) at loading stage, 7.0 – 15.9% (maize) and 4.1– 16.1% (beans) at 
the mid stage, and 6.2 – 64.3% (maize) and 15.9 – 66.7% (beans) at the

3.2. Actual nutrient contents 
3.2.1. Baseline macronutrient and mineral compositions 
The contents of crude protein (8.9 ± 1.1%), crude fat (4.1 ± 0.5%), 
crude fiber (3.6 ± 0.4%), and crude ash (1.3 ± 0.2) of maize at loading 
stage corresponded well with values reported in the literature (protein: 
7.2– 12.4%; fiber: 1.6– 3.8%; fat: 3.1– 5.7%; ash: 1.1– 2.1%) 
(Heuz´e et al., 
2017). Likewise, the contents of protein (23.1 ± 1.0%), fat (1.6 ± 0.2%), 
fiber (5.2 ± 0.4%), and ash (3.9 ± 0.3%) of the beans were within

4.3– 7.9%; ash: 4.0– 6.5%) (Heuz´e et al., 2015). The available carbohy-

USDA, 
2019). The mineral contents were within the range reported by others

higher levels of all the minerals; the contents were higher by factors of 3 
(total minerals), 21 (Ca), 9 (Mn), 5 (Cu, K), 3 (Fe, Zn), 1.7 (Mg), and 1.2 
(Na).

Table 1 shows the influence of different kinds of damage on the

3.3.3. Nutrient abundance prediction models 
There were statistically significant monotonic associations between 
TD and abundance of all the nutrients except Mg, Ca, and Mn in maize, 
and protein, fiber, carbohydrate, calories, and Cu in beans (see supple-
mentary material, Table ST1). All the associations were inverse except 
for Fe (maize). Linear and cubic models fitted the associations well 
(
Fig. 11), but generally, cubic models provided better fits. Linear models 
provided equally good fits for protein, carbohydrate, and calories, while 
cubic models better explained crude fat, fiber, ash (maize), and min-
erals. For instance, with maize, fat and K abundance increased with TD 
up to ~40% damage, then decreased and increased again at damage 
levels >80%. Fe (maize) and Cu (beans) abundances also increased at 
higher damage levels.

and rodents) and discoloration, may continue in the post-harvest period, 
especially if the grain is poorly dried, handled, or stored. Of the damage 
types investigated, insect damage progressed more resolutely. While 
improved practices prevented heavy insect damage, conspicuous dam-
age levels persisted, particularly on the beans, at the late stage. Our 
earlier findings showed that insect damage of beans increased with time 
in air-tight bags because susceptible varieties allowed hidden in-
festations right from the field, which slowly developed and damaged the 
bags weakening their effectiveness (
Mutungi et al., 2020). On average, 
improved practices decreased grain damage and losses 2 – 4-fold. How-
ever, the curvilinear relationship between damage and losses suggested 
that a simple attribution of losses to damage is difficult to conclude. 
Frequently, physical damage causes weight loss, but weight gain or 
minimal weight change also occurs if the damage involves moisture 
gain, fungal infection, pests that burrow into and fail to exit the grain, or 
biochemical changes such as on the seed coat causing discoloration. In 
addition, consumers do not always reject physically defective grains, as 
considered in the losses assessment method applied for this study. These 
dynamics might explain the non-linear component of the association 
between damage and the measured loss magnitudes. 
Insects caused about three-quarters of the damage determined on 
maize and beans. It was of interest that insect damage correlated 
significantly with the contents of most of the nutrients— positively with 
protein (beans), fat, and total minerals but negatively with fiber (maize) 
and carbohydrates. Other authors found higher protein content in 
infested maize and attributed the observation to the disproportionate 
reduction of the starchy components by the feeding bugs, which 
concentrated the nitrogen-containing molecules (
Stathers et al., 2020). 
The association between insect damage and protein content of maize 
was not significant, although an increase occurred at the mid 
post-harvest stage. Different insect pests alter the nutrient concentra-
tions in their substrates differently due to differences in their coloniza-
tion habits and feeding behaviors. In the present study, a pest complex

would concentrate the protein, fat, and minerals found predominantly in 
the germ, while P. truncatus and R. dominica would diminish the car-

inside the grain can alter the nutritional profile of grains (
Rumpold and 
Schlüter, 2013). Moreover, some studies reported an age-related feeding 
behavior such as that of P. truncatus changing from the endosperm in 
early larval instars to the germ in later instars (Vowotor et al., 1998). 
The positive association between insect damage and the protein 
content of beans observed in the present study contradicted other 
findings that reported lower protein content in bruchid-infested beans 
than wholesome ones (
Nta et al., 2019 ). Protein and carbohydrate in 
bean cotyledons are evenly distributed. For this reason, the extraction of 
parts by feeding bruchids may not change the concentrations of these 
nutrients imposingly as in maize. The monotonic increase in protein 
content of beans in our case may have been due to a combination of 
factors, including the presence of insect forms or tissue fragments and 
by-products of nitrogen metabolism. Feeding insects convert host sub-
strates into protein- and fat-rich deposits. Indeed, some authors (
Rum-
bos et al., 2020 ) reported rich protein, fat, and mineral profiles in 
stored-products insect pests. Unlike maize weevils that exit the grain 
immediately, bruchids can hibernate for a while inside the bean seeds 
when the external conditions are unfavorable. Thus, multiple larvae, 
pupae, or adults trapped inside the bean seeds would increase protein 
and fat content. This argument seems plausible since there was no 
deliberate action to remove insect forms buried inside the grains before 
nutritional analysis. Moreover, protein contents were determined based 
on total nitrogen. The total nitrogen probably also included non– protein 
nitrogen and by-products of protein metabolism such as uric acid. 
Jood 
and Kapoor (1993) concluded that highly infested wheat, maize, and 
sorghum seeds exhibited higher protein content due to increased 
non-protein nitrogen, while insect feeding, in reality, decreased the true 
protein content. 
The progressive decrease of carbohydrate and fiber (maize) contents 
with insect damage concurred with the finding of others. 
Jood et al. 
(1993) observed 8– 64% starch loss in maize, whereas Nta et al. (2019) 
reported 28 – 31% carbohydrate loss in beans. The decreases in 
carbohydrate contents were due to endosperm/cotyledon extraction as 
the insects need energy for growth, reproduction, and other processes. In 
addition, an increase in fat and nitrogen-containing constituents 
potentially diluted carbohydrate and fiber contents. Insects buried in-
side the grains can contribute to fiber as chitin contained in their tissues 
is quantified together with fiber (
Kaya et al., 2015). In the present case, 
the fiber contents of beans did not change, implying a balance between 
component extraction and the possible bio-enrichment from the indi-
gestible insect constituent. 
Positive correlations of Fe, Zn, Mn, Ca, Mg, and K contents in maize 
with insect damage contradicted the findings reported by others. 
Jood 
et al. (1992) reported decreases in Ca (4 – 42%), Fe (2 – 39%), Zn 
(2– 18%), Cu (1– 23%), and Mn (2– 16%) in Trogoderma granarium Everts 
(Coleoptera: Dermestidae) (germ feeder) and R. dominica (endo-
sperm/germ feeder) infested grains under laboratory conditions. Simi-
larly, our results opposed the findings by 
Stathers et al. (2020), which 
indicated lower Zn and Fe in maize infested with S. zeamais and 
P. truncatus under laboratory conditions. More than 80% of the minerals 
in maize grains are contained in the germ, and the rest are in the peri-
carp (Fe, Zn) and aleurone layer (
FAO, 1992 ). Severe endosperm 
extraction by endosperm feeders (e.g., S. zeamais ) would therefore 
concentrate the minerals contained in the germ, whereas heavy reduc-
tion of the endosperm and germ by the dual feeders (e.g., P. truncatus 
and R. dominica) would concentrate the minerals found in the pericarp, 
e.g., Fe and Zn. The increase in mineral contents of maize suggests a 
concentration effect arising from endosperm extraction. This effect ap-
pears to have occurred in the beans as the Mg, K, and Na concentrations 
also increased monotonically with insect damage. However, the con-
centration of other minerals (Fe, Ca, Mn, Zn) did not change; hence the 
concentration effects did not apply to all minerals. Similar increases in K 
(and also Zn and Cu) but decreases in Mg and Na (alongside Ca, Fe, and 
Mn on naturally infested beans were reported (
Nta et al., 2019 ). Min-
erals in beans are evenly distributed in the germ (4.5%), endosperm 
(3.6%), and seed coat (4.0%), although the germ is only 1% of the bean 
seed compared to the endosperm (90%) and seed coat (9%). Accord-
ingly, the concentration effects arising from removing parts of the cot-
yledons on total minerals would be less pronounced. Genotype and 
environment also influence the accumulation of specific minerals in the 
various parts of the bean seed (
Ribeiro et al., 2012 ). Interactions 
involving infestation level and these factors might explain our results ’ 
contradiction from those of others.

maize. However, as expected, MD correlated negatively with the crude 
fiber content of the beans. In addition, MD in beans correlated positively

grains are likely to have been immature and poorly filled at harvest time.

the kernel matures while the crude protein, fiber, and total minerals

McDonald, 1983). The effects of shriveling were more profound in the

carbohydrate, fiber, and minerals – Fe, K, Mg, and Na. Protein, fat, and

A significant determinant of nutritional security is access to adequate 
food, i.e., food that is not spoiled or contaminated and is sufficient in 
quantity and nutritional content to meet individuals ’ daily nutrients 
requirements. Households utilize maize and beans conjunctively in some 
parts of East and Southern Africa. As computed in this study, loss- 
adjusted nutrient abundance provided a measure of the nutrient 
amounts that may be accessible at different post-harvest stages, taking 
into account practical features of maize and beans’ post-harvest losses. 
There were marked differences in the nutritional value of maize and 
beans under improved and ordinary practices. Considering the beans, 
the abundance gains on Fe (24%), Zn (12%), Mn (27%), and Cu (34%) at 
the mid stage of the post-harvest season with no gains on protein or 
calories (see 
Fig. 9 ) point to only a minimal influence of improved 
practices on the physical quality of the beans in the short-term. Loss- 
causing damage on harvested grain is unlikely to occur in the first 3 – 4 
months after harvest (
Henckes, 1994; Stathers et al., 2008 ), although 
hidden infestations and intrinsic biochemical changes affecting nutrient 
contents can take place. Conversely, the significant abundance gains on 
all the nutrients at the late stage were due to comparatively lower visible 
damage and removal of dry matter by loss agents under the improved 
practices. For the maize, however, improved practices resulted in more 
gains on protein (mid stage: 13.9%; late stage: 34%), calories (mid stage: 
4.7%; late stage: 40.2%), and minerals (e.g., Zn: 42%, Ca: 105%, Mg: 
38%, and K: 30%), particularly at the late stage. 
The differences in nutritional gains associated with improved post- 
harvest practices on maize and beans show that structure and nutrient 
distribution differences alongside dissimilarities in the kinds of damage 
inflicted by various physical and biological agents have different im-
pacts on different commodities. Hence this study demonstrated the dy-
namic nature of nutritional food loss. Common beans have a more 
homogenous distribution of nutrients than maize (
FAO, 1992; Stathers 
et al., 2020 ), and multiple infesting bruchids tend to lodge inside the 
seeds, unlike maize weevils/borers that exit the grain shortly after 
molting to search for new hosts and mates. Beans also continue to un-
dergo biochemical changes that affect nutrient concentration and

Our findings showed that significant nutrient amounts are lost where 
improved post-harvest practices are not applied. Based on Tanzania ’s 
national per capita consumption data, maize and beans do not satisfy the 
RDAs for calories, protein, and important minerals (e.g., zinc and iron), 
particularly for the adult and pre-adult household members. On average, 
they contribute 52%, 36%, and 8 – 75% of the protein, calories, and 
essential minerals RDAs, respectively. The gaps would, at best, be closed 
from the consumption of other food sources produced on family farms 
(see 
Fig. 1). However, a host of natural, social, human, and economic 
constraints limit rural farmers ’ capacity to diversify farm production 
(Kissoly et al., 2020). The avoided nutrient losses corresponded to 21 g 
and 15.4 g of protein for every kg of harvested maize and beans, 
respectively, with improved post-harvest practices. In terms of calories, 
the avoided losses equated to 932 kcal per kg of maize and 256 kcal per 
kg of beans at the late stage of the post-harvest season. The nutrient 
savings would be significant considering the households ’ low farm 
production levels and narrow crop diversity. 
The extra days of nutrient sufficiency contextualized in the present 
study are significant for grain-deficit households. During the lean sea-
son, food stocks dwindle, and low-income families skip meals, which can 
have serious long-term consequences, particularly for vulnerable 
groups, including children, women, and the elderly. One in every three 
under-five-year-old children in Tanzania suffers from one or multiple 
forms of anthropometric failure (
Khamis et al., 2020 ), while micro-
nutrient deficiencies are rampant across various population groups 
(
Ritchie and Roser, 2017 ). The estimated equivalent days of extra cal-
ories and protein (IQR: 15– 55 days; median: 29– 31 days) from improved 
post-harvest practices signify the potential for alleviating protein/e-
nergy malnutrition associated with poor weight gain and slow linear 
growth in children. Likewise, the equivalent days of extra minerals, 
including Zn (median: 36; IQR: 16– 66), Mg (median: 91; IQR: 45– 166), 
Cu (median: 45; IQR: 23– 81), and K (median: 15; IQR: 7– 26), connote a 
potential for reducing deficiencies and the attendant effects associated 
with these micronutrients. Zn deficiency is especially a leading risk to 
human health in sub-Saharan Africa and is essential for body immunity, 
cell division, and proper growth. Mg is vital for normal nerve and muscle 
function and supports a healthy immune system; K regulates cellular 
fluid balance and supports muscle and nervous system function; while 
Cu is a structural part of many enzymes, supports iron and protein 
metabolism, and is essential for tissue repair, proper functioning of or-
gans, and immune response (
Gharibzahedi and Jafari, 2017). 
One strength of our study is that it examined paired data of physical 
quality and nutrient contents. As applied elsewhere (Spiker et al., 2017; 
Garcia-Herrero et al., 2019), nutritional data from food databases would 
have been used to estimate the nutritional loss associated with the 
post-harvest losses of maize and beans at the farm level. The risk is 
misestimation, as such data do not consider gradual changes in nutrient 
concentrations and the nutritional features associated with food loss 
(
Spiker et al., 2017). Our approach enabled a practical estimation of the 
potential nutritional shifts, considering that the effectiveness of food loss 
interventions exists on a continuum of possibilities because of the het-
erogeneous nature of the socio-economic and biophysical environments 
of the farmers using them. A limitation of the study is that it relied on 
potential gains of nutrient abundance as a proxy for nutritional im-
provements without confirming the impacts on the nutrition status of 
individuals or households. To understand such impact, observations or 
measurements of consumption and bioavailability of nutrients (pro-
portion of nutrients absorbed/metabolized for the physiological benefit 
of the body) would be necessary. Several factors affect nutrient 
bioavailability, including the chemical form, nutrition and health con-
dition of the individuals, and nutrient– nutrient interactions (
Hambidge, 
2010; Gharibzahedi and Jafari, 2017 ). Processing, preparation, and 
cooking methods may also affect nutrient abundance and availability 
(
Oghbaei and Prakash, 2016). It is also worth noting that producers face 
constraints that limit the choice to apply best practices. Economic 
benefits are, in particular, key as producers need enhanced returns to 
pay for the cost of implementing those practices. Our findings suggest

In conclusion, the prevention of nutrient loss post-production is 
especially valuable where food production and diversity to satisfy the 
energy and essential nutrients requirements for healthy sustenance are 
limited. Our results show that nutritional loss is complex and dynamic in 
the face of the many agents that lead to quantitative losses. There are 
varying association patterns between physical quality and the actual 
contents of individual nutrients. Consequently, physically damaged 
maize or beans are not always inferior in nutrient content at the 
compositional level. Improved post-harvest practices generally increase 
the overall quantity of nutrients available for households. The abun-
dance of protein and calories decreases with physical quality loss in both 
maize and beans, and the decrease can be predicted directly from the 
grain damage data using linear models. For the minerals, there are 
mixed trends and more complex interactions. Considering the low farm- 
level production and unmet nutrient demands of households, the avoi-
ded nutrient losses when improved post-harvest practices are used 
amount to significant gains in calories, protein, and minerals (Zn, Mg, 
Cu, and K) in the late post-harvest stage. The benefits are equivalent to at 
least 29– 31 more days of calories and protein sufficiency in every one of 
two households and >55 days for one in four households. Such gains 
have relevance for alleviating under-nutrition at household and com-
munity levels.
