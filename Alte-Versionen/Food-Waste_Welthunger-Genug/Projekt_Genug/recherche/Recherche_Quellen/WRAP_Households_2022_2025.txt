Executive summary 
This report provides estimates of the quantity and types of food and drink waste 
generated by UK households in 2022. The report also looks at the reasons for 
discarding, the financial cost and the greenhouse as (GHG) emission associated 
with wasted food. For more detailed information, including when most food is 
wasted, whether it is discarded in its packaging, and what amounts of various food 
items are wasted in a single instance and why, can be found in the Deep dive into 
household food waste in the UK, 2022 (reasons, patterns and opportunities for 
change) report 1. 
Why are we doing this research? 
Food waste is a global environmental issue, associated with 8-10% of humanity’s 
greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. It has an estimated cost of around US$1 trillion 
annually. In the UK, the majority of food waste is generated in households, 
therefore represent a key priority for efforts to minimise the amount of food waste 
and its environmental impact.  
The results presented in this report will support Governments, WRAP and a wide 
range of organisations working to reduce the amount of food waste from our 
homes. This evidence allows prioritisation of key foods and reasons for discarding 
and assists in strategic decisions. Furthermore, it permits the assessment against 
relevant targets: for the UK Food and Drink Pact (formerly the Courtauld 
Commitment 2030) and Sustainable Development Goal 12.3. Ultimately, this 
research will support efforts to reduce household food waste, bringing benefits to 
individuals, communities, businesses and the UK as a whole.  
This report provides estimates of UK household food (and drink) waste2 for 2022, 
reflecting the timings of fieldwork and measurement.  
How much household food waste is generated?  
In 2022, 6.0 million tonnes of food (and drink) waste was generated from UK 
households. This equates to 88 kg per person per year or 210 kg per household 
per year.

What are the main reasons for food waste? 
The immediate reasons for food being thrown away were obtained from diary 
entries. These suggest that food not used in time, because it either smelled or 
looked off, or was past the date on the label, accounted for approximately 39% of 
wasted food. A further 25% was associated with too much being cooked prepared 
or served. Another 22% was associated with people’s preferences: not wanting to 
eat that element of the food or perceiving it as inedible.

How much does throwing this food away cost us?  
The cost to householders of purchasing food waste was subsequently wasted in 
2022 was £17 billion. This figure is for wasted food – i.e., the edible parts – no 
value has been placed on the inedible parts that are also wasted (such as 
eggshells, animal bones, fruit peel generally perceived as inedible). This figure 
does not include other costs associated with this food in the home (e.g., cooking, 
storage, transport from the shop to home).  
This figure equates to £260 per person each year or £620 per household or £1000 
for a household of four. 
In addition to the above, it cost local authorities approximately £590 million to 
dispose / treat the food waste they collect from UK households in 2022.  
The proportion of food and drink wasted (edible parts)4 in tonnes compared to the 
proportions of wasted food and drink by cost, show some notable differences 
(Figure 4). The higher cost of meals and meat and fish can be observed in the 
greater proportion of these categories in the costs chart, compared to the weight 
of the waste attributed to them. In contrast lower cost per gram categories like 
fresh vegetables and salads, dairy and eggs, and drink make up a greater 
proportion of food waste by weight compared to cost.

What are the environmental impacts of household food waste? 
The GHG emissions associated with wasted food and drink (i.e., edible parts) in 
the UK accounted for approximately 16 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent in 2022. 
This figure includes contributions from the relevant elements of the food and drink 
system: land-use change, agriculture, manufacture, packaging, distribution, retail, 
transport to the home, storage and preparation in the home, and waste treatment 
and disposal. It should be noted that land-use change is inconsistently included by 
sources used in this analysis. This is because of the uncertainty and complexity 
associated with land-use change estimates. Subsequently it is likely that the 
estimate presented here is a slight underestimate. Work in the future will be 
undertaken to understand the emissions associated with land use change from 
wasted food. Details of the methodology are outlined in Section 8.4 of the 
methodology report5.

What methods were used to measure food waste in the home? 
To obtain the estimates in this report, the following pieces of research were 
conducted:  
1. Detailed waste compositional analysis: to classify and quantify food and 
drink waste from local authority collected waste streams. It provides 
detailed information on the amounts and types of food thrown away in these 
waste streams.  
2. General food-waste diaries and sewer-waste diaries: to collect information 
on the reasons for discarding, linked to the types and amounts of food 
waste. Also used to provide an estimate of the total amount of food (and 
drink) waste going down the sewer (e.g., down the kitchen sink, 
dishwasher) and home composted. Fieldwork in 2022 and 2023, covering 
1079 households.  
3. Local authority collected household food waste in the UK 2022: the 
analysis gives an estimate of the total amount of household food and drink 
waste in local authority collected waste streams, comparable over time with 
previous studies. This used data on the quantity of waste in local authority 
waste streams for all authorities in the UK collected via WasteDataFlow, 
collected for the calendar year 2022. Waste composition analysis was also 
conducted to classify food waste collected by local authorities. 193 studies 
were included covering approximately 39,000 households (2022 data). 
Waste composition analysis field work conducted July 2021 and June 2023, 
estimates are pooled to increase the number of studies. 
More details of the methodology used for each element, and on how the data was 
combined, can be found in Methods used for Household Food and Drink Waste in 
the UK 20229.  
What can be done to tackle this issue? 
Household food waste is a systemic issue – while the immediate causes for this 
waste link to food going off, leftovers from cooking and food left on plates, the 
underlying causes are wide-ranging and deep rooted. Where and when we shop, 
how food is sold to us, the design of our kitchens and its appliances, social norms 
around food, our social and work lives – these are just some of the influences on 
food waste in the home.  
To tackle systemic problems, collaborative approaches are required. WRAP is 
working with a wide range of stakeholders to fundamentally change the journey of 
food to ensure substantially less is wasted in the home. This whole system

Glossary 
• Discard route – the method by which household food waste is removed 
from the home after it has been discarded by the households’ occupants. 
Previously referred to as “disposal route”, now changed to reflect that not 
all actions of discarding food in the home result in disposal; home 
composting and anaerobic digestion are productive uses of food waste.  
• Edible parts – see (wasted) food.  
• Edibility – the distinction between whether an item of food waste is 
considered as edible parts (i.e. wasted food) or inedible parts. This does not 
refer to the state of the item of waste at the point of discarding.  
• Food waste – equal to the sum of edible and inedible parts. Household food 
waste is the material that leaves the home for the following processes: 
composting, anaerobic digestion, incineration, disposal to sewer, or landfill. 
Food waste excludes material subject to waste prevention activities, namely 
redistribution for human consumption or fed to animals.  
• Inedible parts – Components associated with a food that, in a particular 
food supply chain, are not intended to be consumed by humans. Examples 
of inedible parts associated with food could include bones, rinds, and 
pits/stones. “Inedible parts” do not include packaging.  
• Local authority collected waste – in this report, refers to the waste streams 
collected by, or on behalf of, the local authorities from households; those 
containing food waste include kerbside residual waste (the ‘general’ bin) 
and collections targeting food waste (either separate or mixed with garden 
waste), with minor contributions from residual waste from household waste 
recycling centres and contamination of kerbside dry recycling.  
• Sewer – one of the major household discard routes of food waste in this 
report, including material disposed of via the sink, toilet or other inlet to the 
sewer system.  
• (Wasted) food (also referred to as edible parts) – products (or parts of 
products) intended for human consumption. This includes material which is 
still suitable for consumption when it is disposed of (i.e. would be regarded 
as ‘edible’) and that which may no longer be suitable for consumption at the 
point of discarding (for example due to it passing a ‘use by’ date or being 
spoiled). It excludes inedible parts.

1.0 Introduction 
This report provides estimates of the quantity and types of food and drink waste 
generated by UK households in 2022. The report also looks at the reasons for 
discarding, the financial cost and the GHG emissions related to wasted food.  
1.1 Importance of measuring household food waste 
Food that is lost or wasted is associated with 8-10% of humanity's GHG 
emissions10. Current research suggests that, in the UK, household food waste 
forms around 60% of all food waste11. Therefore, household food waste is key for 
reducing the UK’s total food waste and its associated environmental impacts. 
Measuring food waste is essential to assess trends over time and inform which 
policies and interventions should be taken to reduce food waste. 
This report provides estimates of UK household food (and drink) waste12. Most of 
the estimates in this report are for 2022, reflecting the timings of fieldwork and 
measurement. 
This data is invaluable in supporting efforts to i) reduce the amount of food wasted 
from UK households and ii) divert food waste away from the residual bins (going 
to landfill or incineration) and into collections targeting food waste, where they are 
available. Information on the amounts and types of food waste, alongside the 
reasons it is thrown away, supports the wide range of businesses and organisation 
working to reduce the amounts and impacts of food waste. The insights within this 
report support prioritisation and decision making, allowing approaches to tackling 
these issues to be based upon the best available information. The results also 
allow assessment of UK food-waste targets, including those for the UK Food and 
Drink Pact (formerly the Courtauld Commitment 2030) and Sustainable 
Development Goal 12.3, which aim to halve per capita global food waste at the 
retail and consumer level by 2030.  Overall, the aim is to reduce costs for citizens 
and local authorities, improve the resilience of the UK food system by making 
better use of the food we buy, and reducing the impact on the environment. 
For tracking the amount of food waste over time, WRAP has conducted a number 
of syntheses of local authority collected household food waste studies since 2007, 
which quantifies the total amount of local authority collected food waste in the UK.

As part of the current work, this analysis has been repeated for 2022 (see the 
report Local Authority collected household food waste in the UK, 202213). 
For an in-depth understanding of household food waste – including the types of 
food that are wasted, why they are wasted, and their disposal route – more 
detailed food waste studies were conducted in 200714 and 2012 by WRAP15. Again, 
this approach has been replicated for 202216, and the results are found within this 
report. This rich data covers food and drink found in waste streams collected by 
local authorities, as well as that home composted or disposed of via the sewer 
(e.g., down the kitchen sink). Additionally, estimates of the environmental and 
financial implications of household food waste are presented. Even more detailed 
information, including when most food is wasted, whether it is discarded in its 
packaging, and how much of different food types are wasted in individual 
instances and why, can be found in the Deep Dive into Household Food Waste in 
the UK, 2022 (Reasons, patterns and opportunities for change)17. 
This current report replaces the report previously published in 2023 (no longer 
available) - Household Food and drink Waste in the United Kingdom 2021/22. The 
reasons for this update were:  
• Food waste was changing rapidly from 2021 to 2022, so presenting data for 2022 
will be more useful into the future.  
• The 2022 data provides more consistency of timing with the data collected for the 
report (food waste diaries and detailed waste composition analysis (see Section 
1.2), which was clustered around 2022. 
• Some inconsistencies in how the amount of food waste was calculated were found 
in the calculations for 2021 data. It was decided to recalculate the data and, rather 
than articulate data for 2021/22, present the data for 2022 for the reason above.  
In 2018, the household food waste results for 2012 were restated18. The changes 
made were to reflect new guidelines to how food waste was being reported

1.3 Reporting of trends over time and UK total food waste  
This research presents trend data over time of household food waste collected 
only by local authorities in the residual stream and in targeted food waste 
collections. The composting and sewer waste data is collected via food diaries, 
which is not deemed robust enough for trend analysis. Therefore, the amount of 
food waste used to track trends over time will be lower than the total UK quantity 
of household food waste. This is a different approach to that adopted when 
reporting household food waste trends over time in previous WRAP reports, which 
have sewer and home composting data available20.  
The reason for this change in approach is because of the suspected 
underestimation and unreliability of diary data to assess trends over time. A study 
by WRAP (Quested et al. 2020) suggested that the levels of underestimation are 
not stable and vary depending on context and the design of the diary. Section 7 of 
the methodology report21 details how the level underestimation is calculated, and 
the adjustment options explored and used in this study. The key takeaway is that 
different diary studies can have different levels of underestimation through minor 
changes in methodology or the context in which they are applied. This makes 
comparisons of diary data between years unreliable.

1.4 Food waste definitions  
In this report, the same definitions of food waste as in the household food waste

international definitions of food waste that have been published subsequently 
(e.g., the Food Waste Index 202124).

• Food waste: food and the inedible parts of food removed from the food 
supply chain (or household) to be recovered or disposed of (including - 
composted, anaerobic digestion, incineration, disposal to sewer or landfill).

This section presents trend data for UK household food waste from the year of a

2.1.1 Overview of trend data presented 
For robust comparisons over time, this trend data only includes food (and drink) 
waste29 collected by local authorities from households. Specifically, it includes the 
waste streams where the vast majority of household food waste is found: the 
“general” waste stream (kerbside residual waste) and collections targeting food 
waste (separate food-waste collections and mixed garden / food waste 
collections).  
The estimates in this section omit household food waste disposed of via the sewer 
and home composting. The reasoning for this is that estimates for household food 
waste disposed of via the sewer and home composting are obtained from diary 
research, which is susceptible to underestimation. The degree of underestimation 
can vary, making comparisons over time problematic. Therefore, for trend analysis 
the focus is on waste streams collected by local authorities, which represent c. 
84% of the total household food waste.  
Also omitted are waste streams collected by local authorities containing little food 
waste: contamination of dry recycling, and waste taken to household waste 
recycling centres (HWRCs). There is insufficient data to reliably track these 
elements over time.  
This means that results tracking food waste in this section are consistent with 
previous ‘Local authority collected household food waste in the UK’ reports30  but 
lower than WRAP publications quantifying total household food waste in the UK31. 
In this section, the same waste streams are being compared over time.

2.1.3 Explanation of trends in household food waste  
A large part of the increase in food waste between 2018 to 2021 is likely due to the 
Covid-19 pandemic and food prices leading to an increase in food purchases for 
consumption in the home, putting upward pressure on household food waste. 
Following this, in 2022, household food waste in the UK then decreased, as food 
prices increased and people spent less time at home again. 
Covid-19 pandemic  
The Covid-19 pandemic explains some of the increase in household food waste 
from 2018 to 2021: restrictions meant people spent more time at home and 
subsequently consumed and wasted more food in the home. 
In 2021, nations in the UK had slightly different restrictions and timelines, although 
across the UK there were lockdowns at the start of the year and restrictions eased 
around summer32 33. During the year, more people continued to work from home 
compared to before the pandemic, with over 40% of working adults in Great 
Britain working from home in the previous seven days, from 7th Jan to 4th April 
2021 (Figure 10). Figure 10 also shows data from Google’s Community Mobility 
Reports. This data tracks the amount of time people were spending in residential 
spaces, and although the figures dropped after the end of national restrictions, the 
figure remained 5-10% higher than the baseline (Jan-Feb 2020) throughout the 
year. Therefore, data from both the ONS and Google indicates people spent more 
time in their homes in 2021, which means it is likely more food was consumed and 
wasted in the home.  
By 2022, people began to move back to working in offices and spent less time at 
home, leading to decreased consumption of food within households and 
subsequently decreased household food waste. In March 2021, 31% of people 
worked from home only and 53% travelled to work at least some of the time, 
whereas in March 2022, 12% of people worked from home only and 71% travelled 
to work at least some of the time34.  
Food waste did not return to pre-pandemic (2018) levels, this could be attributed 
to sustained changes in working habits since the pandemic; in spring 2022, 38% 
of working adults reported working from home at some point within the past seven 
days, compared to 12% before the pandemic35. This three-fold increase indicates

Summary: Key explanations for trends in household food waste 
The levels of household food waste in 2021 could have been affected by the 
Covid-19 pandemic that was on-going at that time. More food was consumed in 
the home during this year compared to pre-pandemic years (and less consumed 
outside the home), potentially putting upward pressure on levels of household 
food waste. In addition, 2021 was before the cost-of-living crisis took hold; during 
this year, the cost of food relative to average incomes was lower than any time 
over the preceding decade, putting further upward pressure on the levels of 
household food waste. As a consequence of these two trends, there was a high 
volume of food purchases from grocery retailers. These trends could explain the 
relatively high levels of household food waste seen in 2021. The subsequent 
decrease in household food waste is likely to have been caused by an easing of 
Covid-19 lockdown restrictions, meaning more time was generally spent outside 
the home. Additionally, grocery prices have continuously and sharply increased 
since the pandemic. A combination of these factors means that the volume of food 
purchased in the UK has decreased, which is likely to have driven down the levels

2.2 Key statistics of household food waste in 2022 
This chapter presents the total household food (and drink) waste40 in the UK, the 
reasons for discarding, proportion of purchases wasted, the financial cost and 
environmental impact of household food waste. These statistics use data from the 
following disposal routes: 
• Local authority collected food waste: 
o Kerbside residual  
o Targeted food waste collections 
o HWRC (Household Waste and Recycling Centre) 
o Dry recycling 
• Sewer waste 
• Home composting 
This chapter therefore utilises data from a larger range of disposal routes than the 
data used to track household food waste over time as presented in Section 2.1. For 
trend data, only local authority collected food waste from kerbside residual and 
targeted food waste collections is utilised. Trend data estimates therefore exclude 
food (and drink) disposed of down the sewer or home composted, measured 
using diaries, and which exhibit varying degrees of underestimation over time, 
making them insufficiently robust for analysis of trends. In contrast, this section 
includes data from a wider range of disposal routes to enable as complete a 
picture of UK household food waste as possible.

2.2.5 Greenhouse gas emissions of household food waste 
The GHG emissions associated with wasted food and drink in the UK accounted 
for approximately 16 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent in 2022.  
This figure includes contributions from the relevant elements of the food and drink 
system: agriculture, manufacture, packaging, distribution, retail, transport to the 
home, storage and preparation in the home, and waste treatment and disposal. 
Emissions from land use change are inconsistently included in the sources of data 
used in these estimates. Therefore, the estimate presented here is likely to be a 
slight underestimate. Further analysis will be conducted in the future to 
understand the emissions from land use for food products associated with higher 
levels of land use change emissions. Details on the methodology used to calculate 
this figure are in Section 8.4 of Methods used for Household Food and Drink 
Waste in the UK 202246. Background information around comparability to previous 
estimates are in the section below ‘Comparing emissions between years’. 
To put this figure in context, the total GHG emissions relating to consumption in 
the UK (all GHG emissions generated through the supply chain of goods and 
services consumed in the UK, wherever they are produced in the world, as 
opposed to emissions produced within the geographical bounds of the UK) 
amounted to around 705 million tonnes in 202147. At the time of writing there is not 
yet an estimate for 2022, but we might expect it to be comparable to the 2021 
estimate. Thus, food and drink wasted by households accounted for 
approximately 2% of this total.  
Figure 22 shows the proportion of food categories by weight and by their 
contribution to the overall GHG emissions of wasted food and drink. Meat and fish 
contribute the largest proportion of GHG emissions of wasted food (26%). This is 
despite only making up 6% of edible food waste by weight. Meals then contribute 
towards 19% of GHG emissions from wasted food while making up 12% of edible 
food waste by weight, while fresh vegetables and salad contribute 16% of all GHG 
emissions from wasted household food waste, despite making up 28% of edible 
food waste.

The emissions associated with food waste in 2012 and 2022 should not be directly 
compared because of differences in the scope of what the total emissions 
estimates include. In 2012, indirect land use is included in the estimates50. In the

emissions from land use change. This means that 2012 estimates include

larger than the 2022 estimate. 
The emissions estimate for 2012 is also likely to be greater than the estimate for 
2022 partly because of reductions in CO2 equivalent emissions in aspects of the 
food value chain. Specifically, the on-going decarbonisation of the electricity grid 
is likely to have reduced emissions in food manufacture, retail and households. 
Additionally, changes in how food waste is disposed also contributes to the lower 
emissions associated with household food waste in 2022 compared to 2012. 
 
Comparison with GHG emissions in the UK Food System GHG report 
The emissions of household food waste have also been estimated in the UK Food 
System GHG51 report which reports 23 million tonnes of CO2e in 2019. That year, 
23% of the total food system emissions were associated with food that becomes 
waste. Between 2015 and 2022, an average annual reduction of about 3.5mt CO2e 
were observed in WRAP’s GHG emissions model52. The figures in this report 
should not be directly compared as a different methodology is used the UK Food 
System GHG report. In calculations from the UK Food System GHG report a top-
down approach is used rather than a bottom-up approach used for the 2022 
estimates. In a top-down approach, total emissions from stages of food value 
chain, such as total emissions from agriculture, are allocated to food waste. In a 
bottom-up approach, as used in the 2022 estimates, GHG emission factors are 
assigned to each food group and scaled up according to the weight of wasted

2.3.4 Home-made and Pre-prepared Meals

sandwiches, and composite savoury products (e.g. pasties), that can be eaten as a 
snack on their own or as part of a meal.

Oil and Fat 
This group excludes fats (and juices) generated by the cooking of meats in the

edible and cost £110 million (Table 42). Half of the wasted food was discarded due 
to being residue from pan, bowl or packaging, mainly referring to oils, and almost 
one third was discarded due to personal preference or being perceived as inedible

Other 
This group includes unidentifiable food and drink waste and foods that do not fit 
into another category, e.g. baby food, liquids drained from cans and jars. Note that 
non-food items (medicines and pet food) have been removed from the dataset as 
they are not considered human food.  
 
This category largely contains mixed semi-solid food, which is considered to be an 
inedible part as a rule of thumb. The wasted food which is unidentifiable or from 
other mixed, canned and bottled food includes: drainings, baby food and other 
items such as food colouring.  
 
UK households generated 310,000 tonnes of ‘other’ food waste in 2022. Almost a 
third was classified as inedible (Table 44).
