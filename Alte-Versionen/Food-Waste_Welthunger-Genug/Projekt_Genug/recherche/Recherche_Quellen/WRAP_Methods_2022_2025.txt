1.0 Introduction 
This document contains details of the research methods and analysis used to obtain the results 
for Household Food and Drink Waste in the United Kingdom 2022. This replaces the document 
previously published in 2023 (no longer available), which looked at the research methods and 
analysis used to obtain results for Household Food and drink Waste in the United Kingdom 
2021/22. The reasons for this update were:  
 
• Food waste was changing rapidly from 2021 to 2022, so presenting data for 2022 will be 
more useful into the future.  
• Some inconsistencies in how the amount of food waste was calculated were found in the 
calculations for 2021 data. It was decided to recalculate the data and, rather than 
articulate data for 2021/22, present the data for 2022 for the reason above.  
1.1 Background and history 
WRAP have measured household food waste in the UK since 2007. This includes trend data, 
which monitors how the amount of household food waste has changed over time. WRAP research 
estimates the total amount of local authority collected household food waste1, such studies have 
been published by WRAP at least every 2-3 years allowing trends over time to be analysed. More 
detailed food waste studies have been conducted in both 2007 and 20122 which includes a 
detailed waste composition analysis of food waste, diaries research as well as a local authority 
collected household waste study. The inclusion of all of these elements allows the total amount of 
household food waste in the UK to be estimated by including data on food waste which is home 
composted or disposed of via the sewer. Additionally, details of exactly what food is wasted, 
reasons for discarding and the impact of household food waste (environmental and financial) can 
be gained from these more detailed pieces of research.  
 
The 2012 household food waste results were restated in 20153, using the data collected in 2012. 
The primary difference in 2015 compared to 2012 is the categorisation of food as edible and 
inedible instead of unavoidable, possibly avoidable, and avoidable. Additionally, food fed to pets 
was omitted from counting towards household food waste. These 2015 updates to the 
methodology have, therefore, also been applied in this updated research. 
 
Given that 2012 was the last time either a detailed waste composition analysis and food waste 
diaries study was conducted, an update of household food waste data was required. Household

1.4 Food waste definitions  
In this report, the same definitions of food waste as in the household food waste statistics 
restated for 2007 to 2015 have been utilised5. 
The terminology used is with the edibility classification system is as follows: 
• Food waste: Food and the inedible parts of food removed from the food supply chain (or 
household) to be recovered or disposed of (including - composted, anaerobic digestion, 
incineration, disposal to sewer or landfill). This definition excludes waste prevention 
activities, namely redistribution for human consumption, or diverted to feed animals6.  
• Wasted food (also referred to as edible parts): products (or parts of products) intended for 
human consumption. This includes material which is still suitable for consumption when it 
is disposed of (i.e. would be regarded as ‘edible’) and that which may no longer be suitable 
for consumption at the point of discarding (e.g. for example due to it passing a ‘use by’ 
date or being spoiled). It excludes inedible parts.  
• Inedible parts: Components associated with a food that are not intended for human 
consumption. Examples of inedible parts associated with food could include bones, rinds, 
and pits/stones. “Inedible parts” do not include packaging. What is considered inedible

2.0 Rationale for primary 
research data used 
This section outlines the primary research conducted and the rationale for why particular data 
has been used.  
2.1 Primary research conducted 
The research is comprised of the following pieces of primary research: 
 
1. Detailed waste compositional analysis 21/22: This study classifies and quantifies food and 
drink waste from 1,807 households. This gives detailed information on the types of food 
wasted. Additionally, households completed a face-to-face questionnaire covering 
sociodemographic and behavioural questions. Sampling was designed to be 
representative for the UK, with households in England and Wales being sampled. The 
detailed methodology for this part of the research is given in chapter 3.0. 
2. Kitchen and sewer diaries 22/23: Information on the types of food and drink wasted was 
recorded in diaries by a total of 1,079 households (459 sink diaries and 620 Kitchen 
diaries). Two separate diaries were conducted for this research, one diary for sewer waste 
(sink waste) and another for kitchen waste which included food disposed of via the 
residual bin, separate food waste containers, home composted or fed to pets. For each 
instance of waste, the amount of waste, the reason for discarding and which ‘waste’ 
stream was used was recorded. The research included waste streams that are hard to 
measure from compositional analysis (material poured down the kitchen sink or other 
inlets to the sewer or home composted). Additionally, although no longer classed as food 
waste and not a focus of this work, the diaries also allow an understanding of the amount 
of food fed to animals. Each household was also surveyed before and after the diary 
research via a questionnaire, giving information on attitudes, stated behaviours and 
household characteristics. The detailed methodology for this part of the research is given 
in Chapter 4.0. 
3. Local authority collected household food waste in the UK 2022: This research collates 
recent waste compositional analyses (WCA’s) commissioned by local authorities. The 
information from these audits is used alongside waste and recycling tonnage data from 
WasteDataFlow – a reporting system for waste collected by local authorities in the UK. The 
analysis gives an estimate of the total amount of household food and drink waste in local

authority collected waste streams. See separate report: Local authority collected household 
food waste in the UK 20227, for more details of the methodology and report.  
Timeframe of research conducted 
The overall food waste estimates presented in this report are referred to as being from 2022 
because of the timeframe in which different elements of the research were conducted. The 
majority of data has been collected in 2022, with small amounts collected at the end of 2021 and 
the start of 2023. 
The detailed waste composition analysis was conducted at the end of 2021 and start of 2022, to 
provide the detail around the types of food wasted in local authority collected waste streams. The 
local authority collected household food waste studies, which is used to track the amount of food 
waste overtime utilises more general local authority collected waste composition data from July 
2021 and June 2023 (estimates are pooled from a longer period to increase the number of data 
points). To understand the total amount of waste collected from local authorities for the local 
authority collected household food waste studies, data from 2022 was used from WasteDataFlow. 
At the time of conducting this research this was the most recent data available. The diaries were 
then conducted at the end of 2022 and start of 2023, with one wave of research in each year. 
Subsequently some of the data used to understand total food waste from home composting and 
sewer waste as well as reasons for discarding is from the start of 2023. 
 
2.2 Rationale for research used: Food and drink waste 
collected by local authorities  
Total amount of food wasted in the UK 
In this study the local authority collected household food waste in the UK studies method was 
used to estimate the total amount of household food waste collected by local authorities (LAs), 
which is in line with the methodology used in previous years8. Besides the local authority collected 
household food waste in the UK studies method, for estimating LA collected waste other options 
include:

• Scaling the information from the detailed waste compositional analysis: the average 
amount of food and drink waste per household in these waste streams can be calculated 
and scaled up to the total number of households in the UK.  
• Scaling the average amount of food and drink waste per household in the kitchen diaries 
associated with these waste streams to the whole UK population.  
The local authority collected household food waste in the UK studies method has a number of 
advantages for calculating the total amount of food and drink waste: 
1. It uses a larger number of compositional studies already performed by local authorities to 
derive waste estimates. This means that the effective sample size of the study is much 
higher than either of the other two methods. 
2. It uses information in WasteDataFlow in combination with waste composition data, thus 
meaning that the estimates are partially based on a census of the total amount of material 
in relevant waste streams. For streams where all the waste is likely to be food and drink 
(i.e. separate food waste collections), the information is not subject to sampling errors – 
again the uncertainty in the results is much lower.  
3. Another strength of the method is that general waste audits undertaken by local 
authorities usually have less contact with the households that they are sampling from. 
Specifically waste audits from local authorities do not typically include a questionnaire 
before a waste audit, as is the case with the detailed waste compositional analysis and the 
kitchen diaries. This means that the degree to which households change their behaviour 
as a result of any contact is likely to be smaller which may result in lower participation bias. 
4. Finally, because the data does not need to be linked to households, it is easier for the 
waste composition data to include flats and other shared properties, allowing the 
estimates to be more representative of the UK population of all households.  
A potential disadvantage of using the local authority collected household food waste studies 
method is that it relies on the accuracy of data submitted on WasteDataFlow by local authorities. 
WasteDataFlow is a reporting system for waste collected by local authorities in the UK, in which 
local authorities have to report all their waste data into. Although WasteDataFlow is a well-
established database of local authority collected waste and checks are undertaken on data 
submitted it may contain some inaccuracies. For example, waste data submitted as household 
waste could mistakenly include waste from schools or small businesses, resulting in an 
overestimate of household food waste.  
On the other hand, diary methods suffer from underestimation of the amount of food and drink 
waste. For the food element of the local authority collected waste streams (i.e. omitting food and 
drink waste disposed of to the sewer and home composted), a comparison between diary 
research and detailed composition analysis was performed on data from WRAP for 2007 and

Types of food wasted 
The detailed waste composition analysis is used to understand the types of food wasted in local 
authority collected waste streams. Although the detailed waste compositional analysis suffers 
from some drawbacks in obtaining the total amount of food and drink waste, these drawbacks 
are less important when being used to determine the proportion of different types of food and 
drink thrown away. In detailed waste composition analysis, the precision of data collected creates 
logistical challenges and increases the required time for sampling. However, a sample of 1807 
households used in this study still provides a large enough sample size to understand the types 
of food wasted by UK households. As a waste composition analysis is a sample of the population, 
random sampling errors are inevitable, however these are accounted for at the nation level in the 
confidence intervals presented in section 9.0. Systematic errors in sampling can then occur if the 
households sampled are not representative of the population. Therefore, weightings have been 
applied to the waste composition data to account this (see section 6.0). 
Why food is wasted? 
Despite the shortcomings of diary-based research, the kitchen diaries are the only reliable source 
of information on the reasons why food is thrown away. As in 2012, the kitchen diaries will be 
used to estimate reasons for discarding for all waste streams in the research. The diaries enable 
the reasons for discarding for each food item wasted to be specified, the diaries include a 
question on why something was thrown away, what the item is and the quantity. Therefore, 
reasons for discarding by food category can be determined. Using a questionnaire would only 
allow a more general assessment of why food is thrown away or it would be reliant on someone 
thinking back to the last time they threw something away. Subsequently, the data collected from a

2.3 Rationale for research: sewer and home 
composted food and drink  
This section outlines why diaries research has been used for both sewer and home composted 
waste. It should be noted that separate diaries have been used for quantifying sewer and home 
composted food waste. As in the restated household food waste for 2007-2015 report10, food fed 
to animals is no longer considered food waste. Therefore, although diaries record this data this is 
not a focus of this report and so is not discussed specifically in this section. 
Total amount of food wasted in the UK 
Data for food and drink disposed of via the sewer has been collected via diaries focusing only on 
sewer waste. This approach is aligned with the methodology used in the Down the Drain11 study, 
which also utilised a separate diary for sewer waste only. A focused diary approach which looked 
only at sewer waste intended to avoid the need for participants to report details on multiple 
disposal routes which should mean that there is less of an underestimation. This was an issue 
identified with the diaries conducted in the 2012 equivalent study, in which diaries asked 
participants to report on multiple disposal routes.  
 
Data for the amount of food waste home composted utilises a diary method which is the only 
feasible method for obtaining information on the amount of food and drink waste that is home 
composted. Other methods have been considered (e.g. compositional analysis of a compost 
heap), but major problems exist with such methods (e.g. in the example above, knowing when 
food was placed on the compost heap). 
 
Diaries, as discussed above, suffer with issues with underestimation, although it is unclear the 
level of which this exists sewer waste and home composting as there are few (if any) comparable 
studies. The degree of underestimation may be substantially less than for local authority collected 
waste: if diaries influence behaviour, then there is the possibility that material is diverted towards 
disposal routes that are seen as socially desirable (such as home composting), which could 
counteract any incompleteness of diary entries.

3.0 Methods for detailed waste 
compositional analysis  
This section of report details the methodology of the detailed waste composition analysis 
component of this project. A methodology is outlined for the sampling process, the questionnaire 
conducted and details around the waste composition analysis.  
3.1 Overview  
The aim of the waste composition analysis was to classify and quantify food and drink waste from 
local authority collected waste streams. In addition, the project aimed to provide detailed 
information on the demographics and behaviours of each household sampled in the research. 
 
To achieve these aims a detailed food waste specific compositional analysis on the waste streams 
of a sample of households, representative of the UK was undertaken. In addition, a questionnaire 
related to food, food waste, demographic information and behaviours was conducted for the 
same households prior to the waste compositional analysis (WCA). Data from this questionnaire 
will be used by WRAP in future research to understand demographic and behavioural drivers of 
household food waste. For example, the questionnaire will allow an exploration of whether 
factors such as age, household size and income impact food waste. Additionally, an assessment 
of behaviours can be analysed such as around food storage and meal planning. At the end of the 
questionnaire, informed consent was sought; all households taking part in the waste 
compositional analysis provided informed consent for their waste to be collected and analysed. 
Any information collected about the householder was treated as strictly confidential. The 
householder’s details were not linked to the waste collected and it is not possible to identify the 
household within any published data.  
 
The fieldwork was undertaken by Resource Futures (WCA) and Censuswide (questionnaire) 
November 2021 and March 2022. The two anonymised datasets (from the questionnaire and 
WCA) were linked using a unique identification number for each household.  
 
The following section describes the detail of the methodology for the waste compositional 
analysis.  
3.2 Sample design  
The sampling process aimed at achieving a total sample size of 1,800 households, distributed 
within 12 local authorities. The sample was designed to be representative of UK households with 
regard to several factors. To achieve this, a three-stage sampling design was created: firstly local 
PAGE 18
Methods used for Household Food and Drink Waste in the UK 2022 
  
18 
 
authorities were selected, then collection rounds within these local authorities, and, finally, 
households within those collection rounds.  The following sections describe the process, including 
the selection criteria, for each stage of this process in order to yield a sample that was 
representative of households across the UK.  
Selecting local authorities to participate  
The fieldwork was undertaken in England and Wales, reflecting the funding for the project. 
However, the sample was selected to be representative of the whole UK (for both the local 
authorities selected and the households within them). To make optimal use of the budget, the 
households in the sample were drawn from 12 local authorities, three in Wales and nine in 
England. This balances the need to have a dispersed sample and the increased logistical costs 
were the sample to be fully dispersed across the UK.  
 
For the local authorities, a number of criteria were used to guide the selection:  
• Include authorities with a mix of waste collection schemes – especially residual waste and 
any collections targeting food waste – that was representative of those in operation across 
the UK, as shown in Table 1 below;  
• Include a range of deprivation levels and population density that is broadly representative 
of the UK; and  
• Include authorities in England and Wales.  
 
Groups of local authorities were formed using this information, from which local authorities could 
be randomly selected for inclusion in the detailed waste composition study.  Specifically, local 
authorities were stratified by UK nation (England or Wales), waste collection scheme (i.e. 
frequency of food waste and residual collections), density and deprivation. Population density was 
described by the number of people per hectare for each local authority. Local authorities were 
then grouped into three quantiles. Similarly, deprivation was calculated by appending the average 
score for the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) for each local authority; deprivation was also 
grouped into three quantiles.  
 
The groups of local authorities containing the majority of LAs were then used to create the 
sample of LAs representative of the UK for the WCA (Table 2). Some groups of LAs were not 
included because they contained very few LAs therefore their inclusion would result in a sample 
which is not representative of the LAs in the UK. 
 
WRAP contacted the randomly selected local authorities within each group, ensuring that there 
was agreement to participate in each group. Resource Futures followed up to obtain their 
permission to conduct the survey and waste composition analysis in their area.

Selecting collection routes and properties  
Once the local authorities had agreed to participate in the survey, the project team gathered 
information about the waste collection routes, working with the LA to identify the most suitable

use of post-weighting on the dataset.

3.3 Questionnaire survey fieldwork  
A face-to-face doorstep survey approach was adopted for the fieldwork. Previous research (The 
Food We Waste & Household Food and Drink Waste in the UK 2012) adopted an in-home, face-to-
face approach.  A face-to-face approach helped to ensure high rates of response and consent. In 
particular, a face to-face approach encourages consent to the waste composition analysis phase 
and allows the recruitment to this stage of research to be formalised.  
 
The questionnaire took no longer than 25 minutes to conduct. A £5 shopping voucher was gifted 
to residents taking part in the survey to thank them for their participation. Survey fieldwork was 
conducted during the day and in the evening, along with weekend and weekday shifts to ensure 
that a cross-section of respondents participate in the research. Only those who are either solely 
or mainly responsible for food shopping or food preparation were included; those participants 
which were not responsible have not been included in the study. 
 
In total, 3,380 interviews were carried out across twelve local authority areas. Of these, 2,919 
consented to have their waste analysed. It was not possible to evenly distribute the interviews 
across all 12 authorities taking part due to the constraints of time, season and ongoing COVID-19 
pandemic restrictions and associated staff shortages. The number of interviews conducted aimed 
to exceed the required number of households required for the waste composition analysis (1800

householder’s details were not linked to the waste collected as unique codes for households

who also deleted them from their records once the study was complete. Where information has 
been passed to WRAP for the analysis in this report, it was anonymised, and it is not possible to 
identify individual households from this information.

waste compositional analysis collected and analysed food waste from consenting households

Collection of kerbside sample materials  
For each household participating in the research, the waste streams collected and analysed were:  
• kerbside residual waste; and  
• any kerbside organic waste which is intended to include food (i.e., either separate food 
waste collections, or garden waste collections that accept food waste).  
These two waste streams constitute the vast majority of household food waste collected by local 
authorities. A smaller proportion of food waste is also collected by local authorities as 
contamination in dry recycling and within HWRC (Household Waste and Recycling Centres) 
residual waste (see figure 2 in section 3.1 of the report: Local authority collected household food 
waste in the UK 202216). Therefore, the composition of food waste collected by local authorities is 
well represented by sampling kerbside residual and kerbside streams targeting food waste.  
 
Where collection of either residual or organic material was fortnightly, all relevant waste streams 
were collected and analysed over a fortnight period to take account of any variation in arisings 
between the two weeks. Where both waste streams were collected every week, the material from 
these households was collected during just one week.  
 
The route and approximate timings of the collection were agreed with the local authorities in 
advance (see ‘Selecting collection routes and properties’ for details of the selection). Materials 
were collected from the target list of households derived from the survey stage of the research.  
Each day, a team of Resource Futures operatives visited the sample points and collected material 
in a non-compacting vehicle. The collection crew followed the sample round, as marked on their 
collection sheet, which were structured to ensure that the collection crew arrived well before the 
usual material collection, but not so early that households setting out their waste late were 
missed. A record was made of materials collected from each household, by waste stream, on the 
sample collection record sheet. Households were included if they had presented either residual 
waste or food waste in week 1. If in week 1 no waste was presented from a household originally 
targeted for collection another household was included instead. If in the second week of sampling 
a household did not present any waste, then no additional households were sampled. The 
material from each stream and each household was bagged separately, and marked with a tag to 
identify material stream, and with the household ID and number of bags (e.g. one of two) marked 
clearly on the tag, as well as on a label affixed to the bag as a backup.

4.0 Methods for Kitchen and 
Sewer Diary 22/23 research 
This chapter of the report outlines in detail the methodology used to conduct food waste diary 
research. The diaries are used to inform the total amount and types of food wasted via the sewer 
and home composting, as well as the reasons for discarding for all household food waste (Table 
1). As in the restated household food waste for 2007-2015 report18, food fed to animals is no 
longer considered food waste. Therefore, although dairies record this data this is not a focus of 
this report and so is not discussed specifically in this section. 
4.1 Diary research background 
In 2007 WRAP first commissioned food waste dairies, with a single diary used to ask participants 
about food wasted via the sink, home composting and through local authority collected streams. 
In 2008, WRAP commissioned research focusing only on the amount of food and drink disposed 
to sewer – the report was published in 2009 under the name ‘Down the Drain’. In 2012, with a 
view to update the evidence base on all household food and drink waste, a wider kitchen diary 
approach was utilised in the same way as the 2007 diary (i.e. asking respondents to record waste 
to destinations including the sewer, but also their residual bin, any separate food waste caddy 
and home composting etc.). WRAP concluded that this approach was not robust for estimating 
sewer waste as the resulting estimate of food and drink waste going down the sewer was around 
75% lower than the 2008 estimate. Further investigation indicated that the increased burden on 
respondents (from being asked to record food and drink waste for all disposal routes from the 
home) was the most likely explanation of these discrepancies (see section 2.3 in the Methods 
used for Household Food and Drink Waste in the UK 2012 report19). It was therefore concluded 
that in future research, separate diaries should be used to capture waste down the sewer and 
waste in the bin/food caddy/compost.  
At the start of 2022, with the objective of reviewing and updating the methods used to investigate 
food and drink waste to sewer, and the evidence base on household food and drink waste, WRAP 
commissioned a new study. A small pilot was undertaken with WRAP colleagues who tested an 
updated methodology which included a digital-diary prototype and the tried-and-tested pen and 
paper diary previously used.

4.2 Data collection methods  
Recruitment/screening questionnaire  
Before proceeding with recruitment, a screener questionnaire was prepared. Invites were sent via 
an online panel to a selection of individuals, inviting them to complete further screening 
questions, whereupon quotas were monitored to select or screen out respondents.  
This questionnaire included essential screening criteria such as responsibility for food shopping 
and preparation, and availability during the scheduled ‘diary week’. Profiling questions were also 
included, looking at gender, age, location, ethnicity, living situation, details about other household 
members, social grade, and access and use of food and drink waste collection services. These 
profiling questions were included to ensure a good fit with nationally representative quotas.   
Additionally, the screener also asked respondents to indicate their preference for completing 
either a pen and paper or digital diary. In earlier pilots, prompts appeared to those who chose the 
latter to understand their digital confidence. However, as the sample was drawn from online 
panels, there were no anticipated issues with levels of digital competency and as such these 
prompts were removed.  
The questionnaire also asked what, if any, appropriate measuring devices they own to ensure 
arrangements could be made to send either a digital set of scales or a 1 litre capacity measuring 
jug and 100ml capacity beaker before fieldwork began. For project budgeting purposes, a cap was 
set of no more than 50% of each sample (food and sewer waste) to require equipment.

being shown to relevant target respondents.

4.3 Sampling framework 
A hybrid sample framework approach was undertaken which involved inviting a random selection 
of respondents from an online research panel from high-level targetable strata such as UK 
nations, gender and age. Research panels are a practical way to recruit participants who are 
generally willing to participant in research. Although it should be noted that research panels may 
include people who exhibit different types of behaviours than the general population. The 
alternative of recruiting participants from random households in the UK would be impractical 
because of the time needed to onboard new participants and ensure they were representative of 
the UK.  Additionally, there is no evidence to our knowledge which suggests people in research 
panels produce significantly different amounts or types of food waste. Therefore, research panels 
are a practical and appropriate way to recruit participants.

household were used in the sampling design, to ensure the sample is representative. Monitoring 
quotas were then set on other criteria, e.g. household composition, housing type and tenure,

in Birmingham in a mortgaged terraced house with four people in the household). However, a

5.1 Calculations for local authority collected waste  
For the local authority collected waste, the detailed information by food type comes from the 
detailed waste compositional analysis. This includes detailed information on what food is wasted 
and in what proportion, for example, it provides detail on the proportion of waste which are 
vegetables and salads, as well the proportion, which is potato, whether that is whole, mashed or 
just peel. This detail can then be combined with total amount of food waste estimated by the 
Local authority collected household food waste in the UK 2022 analysis. 
 
The waste composition dataset was weighted by the number of occupants in the household as 
described in section 6.2. The weights of food were also seasonally adjusted using the method 
outlined in 6.5. Seasonal factors were calculated to convert information collected in one month of 
the year to an annual figure, as different food items are bought and wasted in different quantities 
through the year.  
 
The total amount of food and drink waste was then scaled so the total amount is equal to the 
estimate from the Local authority collected household food waste in the UK 2022. This scaling 
was necessary as the latter research provides the more robust estimate of the total amount of 
food and drink waste in local authority collected waste streams (see section 2.2 for more details). 
The scaling factor used was 1.24, i.e. estimates in the detailed compositional analysis needed 
increasing by 24%. This is very similar to the scaling factor used for 2012 estimates in Household 
Food and Drink Waste in the UK which used a factor of 1.23.  
 
Households that were interviewed and consented to have their waste collected but did not have 
any waste collected and analysed were excluded from the sample. This decision was made 
because the reasons for the waste not being collected were varied. For instance, some 
households did not present waste at kerbside for reasons that are unknown. For others, it was 
not possible to link the waste to a household; this happened when bins and bags were placed or 
piled together making identification impossible. Had the reasons all been connected with non-
presentation of waste (inferring a waste prevention effect), then there would have been 
advantages to leaving the households in the sample, but recording their food and drink waste as 
zero. However, as this wasn’t always the case, they were excluded.

Diary data processing  
Before analysis of the diary data could be undertaken a number of data processing steps were 
required, as detailed in the following sections. 
Splitting entries 
Firstly, entries were split which contained more than one food item. Participants of the diaries 
were asked to only enter one food item per entry into the diary and to record what was thrown 
away, the quantity discarded and the reasons for discarding. However, a number of entries 
contained information for multiple different food categories. These entries required splitting out 
to allow a record of the weight of each individual food category to be recorded. In cases that 
entries were split it was assumed that the reasons for discarding and disposal route was the 
same for each of the items included in one entry. 
Weight of items  
Where items were recorded in the diary as the number of items or as a volume, these entries 
required converting to a standard weight.  
In cases in which participants recorded the number of items wasted, for example the peel of an 
orange or whole broccoli. Standard weights were determined for such items by using data readily 
from supermarket websites which provide pack weight and serving suggestion information. Data 
was not always available for every item and so proxy values are used in some cases in which 
similar items are used to represent items with missing weight data.  The weights used to for 
different food items are shown in section 10.2 in the appendix. 
To make diary entries as practical as possible for participants, they could record the quantity of 
food or drink wasted using a range of measures, including handfuls, measuring jugs and spoons. 
In these cases the volume of each type of measuring device was used to calculate what was 
wasted in mls. The bulk density of specific types of food items was then used to determine the 
weight of these entries. Bulk density data was collected from a range of sources including 
PAGE 47

47 
 
scientific papers2223, data originating from the FOA24 and data published for use by food 
processes25.  
Calculations relating to water gain and loss  
When certain food is cooked and drink products are made they can gain and loose water. For 
example, pasta and rice absorb water, while meats loose water, water is then added to drinks like 
tea. This a consideration to make when using data relating to the quantity of a food category 
when it is purchased, such as in the calculations outlined in the sections: Percentage of food 
purchased wasted analysis and Food and drink price.  
For products which are cooked, firstly the percentage of the wasted category which is cooked is 
calculated using data from the waste composition analysis which recorded this information for 
every item recorded in the analysis. For this cooked waste, a change in weight is applied from 
factors given in McCance and Widdowson. This gives the equivalent amount of raw food for these 
categories, which is then added to the weight of food thrown away as raw to understand the 
purchase weight of food wasted.  
For products in which water is added, such as for hot drinks (tea and coffee), gravy and stock, the 
amount of water added was estimated using serving suggestion information provided by brands 
and supermarkets. This enabled a proportion of each of these liquids to be calculated as being 
the dry purchase weight of that food/drink category.

• Food waste: Food and the inedible parts of food removed from the food supply chain (or 
household) to be recovered or disposed of (including - composted, anaerobic digestion, 
incineration, disposal to sewer or landfill). This definition excludes waste prevention 
activities, namely redistribution for human consumption, or diverted to feed animals27.  
• Wasted food (also referred to as edible parts): products (or parts of products) intended for 
human consumption. This includes material which is still suitable for consumption when it 
is disposed of (i.e. would be regarded as ‘edible’) and that which may no longer be suitable 
for consumption at the point of discarding (e.g. for example due to it passing a ‘use by’ 
date or being spoiled). It excludes inedible parts.  
• Inedible parts: Components associated with a food that are not intended for human 
consumption. Examples of inedible parts associated with food could include bones, rinds, 
and pits/stones. “Inedible parts” do not include packaging. What is considered inedible 
varies among users (e.g., chicken feet are consumed in some food supply chains but not 
others), changes over time, and is influenced by a range of variables including culture, 
socio-economic factors, availability, price, technological advances, international trade, and 
geography.

When this approach was being designed there were two main considerations to make when 
classifying food categories according to edibility: 
1. How to determine whether a food category is considered edible or not. There was not an 
existing framework or set of guidelines outlining the edibility of food. Therefore, a 
questionnaire was used to understand the culture norms in the UK around what food 
categories are considered edible or not. 
2. A number of food categories are partially edible and partially inedible. For example, a 
banana skin is inedible, but its flesh is edible, so when a whole banana is discarded a 
proportion is edible and a proportion is inedible. Therefore, a particular food category 
needs to be assigned an edibility percentage, to determine what proportion of that 
product is edible.

Determining the edibility of a food item 
To determine if a food category is considered edible or not a questionnaire was used to classify 
food items which are edible and inedible. Two questions were developed, the first asks which 
items / parts of items people actually eat, and the second about which items they consider edible 
and which inedible, whether or not they eat them themselves. The two questions were asked 
about sixteen different parts of items (e.g. apple peel, parsnip peel). These items were chosen 
because a) they represented a substantial amount of waste as previously determined from waste 
compositional analysis and diary research, b) their ‘edibility’ was judged to be ‘borderline’ by the 
authors, and c) they could be used as proxies for other items. 
A score was assigned to the answers given to each question based on the response (e.g. 1 for 
“always”, 1/3 for “occasionally”) and average taken across the respondents’ answers for each item 
to obtain a score for each item for each question. For a given item, the average score was 
calculated for the two questions, providing a single value to reflect its perceived edibility from the 
UK population.

6.0 Weighting and seasonal 
adjustment 
6.1 Objective of weighting in this study 
Weighting of the sample data is used to adjust where the sample does not match the population 
for important variables, in this case variables that correlate with the amount or type of waste 
being thrown away. The use of weighting factors helps to eliminate bias in the results but also 
reduces the effective sample size and leads to worse precision. Weighting changes the relative 
contribution of certain values in a sample, meaning some samples are slightly increased and 
others decreased depending on whether they are over or underrepresented. The effective 
sample size then measures the degree of precision, showing what sample size could have been 
used to provide the sample level of precision. An effective sample size will always be lower than 
the actual sample size, although weighting should be used to create a representative sample 
whilst having as small an impact on the effective sample size as possible. 
 
Different elements of this research have different requirements for weighting because they are 
sampling at different scales:  
• Local authority collected household food waste in the UK 2022: as this work synthesises 
together information from individual local authorities, the key is to ensure that the local 
authorities in the study are as representative of the UK population of local authorities as 
possible (or the geographical area they are representing).  
• Waste compositional analysis and diary research: in these studies, the total amount of 
waste generated by each participating household is measured or estimated. Therefore, 
ensuring the households in the study are representative of the UK population of 
households is the key factor. 
 
6.2 Weighting of Waste Composition Analysis

with the weighting applied to the analysis in 201230. The number of people in the household has 
been demonstrated to correlate strongly with the amount of waste produced in many studies 
(e.g. Household Food and Drink Waste in the UK 2009 and 2012). Within the waste compositional 
analysis, there is an underrepresentation of one-person households which is likely driven by flats 
not being included in the waste composition analysis. Flats often use communal containers,

6.5 Seasonality adjustment  
The amounts and types of food purchased are known to vary seasonally. This is shown in monthly 
data from 2007 to 2020 provided by the Family Food survey. It is likely that more waste is 
generated for a given type of food when more of that food is entering the home. Therefore, 
purchasing data was used the adjust the amount of the amount of specific waste food categories. 
The same methodology was applied as in 2012 increasing comparability between the studies. The 
2012 methodology provides more detail on the impacts of seasonality and how these impacts 
were investigated32.  
 
Both the Waste composition analysis and diaries research requires a seasonal adjustment. The 
waste composition analysis was conducted over a 5-month period (November 2021 and March 
2022). The diaries in this study took place over a 4-month period (November 2022 and February 
2023). Therefore, both of these pieces of research provide a ‘snapshot’ in time of the waste 
produced by households for a particular point in time has therefore been adjusted to ensure the 
data is representative of the whole year. The Local authority collected household food waste in 
the UK analysis does not require a seasonal adjustment as it utilises waste composition data 
collected throughout the whole year and Waste Data Flow tonnages which show annual waste 
arisings.  
 
To adjust food categories according to seasonal variation in food purchases and so waste, Family 
food data was utilised between the years 2007 and 2020. A time series analysis was undertaken, 
with a second-order polynomial trend-line fitted to purchasing data of a particular item. For each 
month, the deviation from this trend-line is calculated as a percentage of the trend-line value for 
the month in question. These deviations are averaged for each month giving the seasonal trend in 
purchases. These trends can in turn be utilised to determine factors that convert estimates of 
waste for one month to an annual estimate. For example, if purchasing levels were 10% lower in 
the month of the year that research was undertaken compared to the annual level of purchasing, 
then the estimate from that month would be divided by 0.9 (i.e. 100% – 10%) to obtain the 
average for the year.

7.0 Diary underestimation 
This chapter outlines why diaries are likely to be under estimating food waste which is home 
composted and disposed of via the sewer. The chapter also assesses how the diary should 
subsequently be used and reported. 
7.1 Why is there underestimation in the diaries? 
Diaries are one of the few options for collecting data on household food waste disposed of via the 
sink and via home composting. Diaries are, however, suspected of underestimating food waste 
compared to waste composition (WCA) studies. A study by WRAP (Quested et al. 202033) 
suggested that the levels of underestimating are not stable and vary depending on context and 
the design of the diary.  
WCA is a more accurate way of measuring waste as researchers can directly measure what is 
being thrown away rather than relying on participants to record their waste, as with diaries. 
However, these methods are problematic for home composting and sewer waste. For example, a 
composition analysis of a compost heap would have issues of knowing when something was 
discarded and as food decomposes it would be hard to identify.

Has sewer and home composted waste declined? 
Taken at face value, the results indicate a decrease in amount of food waste disposed of via the 
sewer (48% compared to 2012) and home composting (68%). This section discusses other data to 
help us understand whether a downward trend has occurred. In summary, whilst small 
reductions could have occurred, any change is likely to be much smaller in magnitude than the 
diaries data suggests.   
 
The apparent decreases would indicate a substantial behaviour change, which is not supported 
by other studies or datasets to our knowledge. For compost waste, the food waste tracker 
surveys suggest a reduction in the proportion of households home composting by 4% percentage 
points between c. 2012 and 2022 (a decrease of around 20%).  
 
For sewer waste, WRAP is currently engaging with water companies to understand if they have 
any evidence for changes in food waste over time. It is understood that water companies have 
run campaigns to reduce food waste down the sink, which may have reduced food waste. 
Potentially, these could have led to a diversion of some food and drink waste (oils & fats, more 
‘solid’ waste) from sewer disposal to LA collection. However, it is hard to get information on the 
prevalence of such campaigns over time or their impacts. It is unlikely that these campaigns 
would have influenced drink waste being poured down the sink or into the dishwasher, as most 
people would not pour those into the bin or separate food-waste collection, so even if they have 
changed behaviours in some households, it is unlikely to have reduced sewer waste on the scale 
seen.  
 
Alternatively, there is the potential that people have reduced the types of waste going down the 
sewer or to home composting (an at-source reduction, rather than diversion to other waste 
streams). This would be unlikely as i) food waste disposed of to these destinations has broadly 
similar reasons to LA collected waste; ii) we would expect to see a corresponding reduction in 
sales of drinks and other relevant food categories: this has not been observed.  
 
The increase in food waste collected by local authorities could also be used as supporting 
evidence for reducing sewer and compost waste. Food waste could have been diverted from the 
sewer and home composting to waste streams collected by local authorities. Food waste 
collected by LAs has increased by 0.3 million tonnes since 2012. However, this increase is more 
likely explained by a number of factors, such as the pandemic changing behaviours and low food 
prices prior to the cost-of-living crisis. The diaries suggest that in total sewer and compost waste 
has decreased by 1.1 million tonnes since 2012, three times the increase in food waste now 
collected by local authorities. Therefore, the reduction in sewer and compost waste cannot be 
explained by the diversion of food waste to local authority waste streams alone.  
 
Overall, there may have been a change in food wasted home composted or going down the 
sewer, but any reduction is highly unlikely to have been of the extent suggested by taking the data 
at face value: this apparent change is not supported by other evidence available. This analysis

The analysis of GHG emissions related to household food waste was conducted with the intention

of showing changes in emissions between years, however this section looks at why the estimate 
for 2022 is likely to vary from previous years. The reasons for differences in GHG estimate 
between years include both methodological differences and differences caused by changes in the 
GHG emissions of food waste.  
Comparison with GHG emissions using 2012 household food waste data

The emissions associated with food waste in 2012 and 2022 should not be directly compared

Comparison with GHG emissions in the UK Food System GHG report 
The emissions of household food waste have also been estimated in the UK Food System GHG48 
report which reports 23 million tonnes of CO2e in 2019. The figures in this report should not be 
directly compared as a different methodology is used the UK Food System GHG report. In 
calculations from the UK Food System GHG report a top-down approach is used rather than a 
bottom-up approach used for the 2021/22 estimates. In a top-down approach, total emissions 
from stages of food value chain, such as total emissions from agriculture, are allocated to food 
waste. In a bottom approach, as used in the 2022 estimates, GHG emission factors are assigned 
to each food group and scaled up according to the weight of wasted food from each group. 
Therefore, in a bottom-up approach the composition of wasted food is used as a basis for 
understanding total GHG emissions.

available. Instead to understand the proportion of different types of food, the composition of 
food purchased is used in the calculations, which varies from the composition of wasted food. For 
example, when looking at the proportion of all purchases by food type, dairy & eggs, drinks and 
confectionary & snacks are bought in higher proportions compared to the proportion they are 
wasted in. These food types are associated with relatively high GHG emissions which may

9.0 Uncertainty 
 
9.1 Uncertainties associated with Local authority 
collected household food waste in the UK 2022 
Compositional Data 
Sampling of households within local authority compositional analyses 
Random and systematic uncertainties 
It is not practically feasible or desirable to perform compositional analysis on all households 
within a local authority, so samples were drawn. Random errors in the results from each 
compositional audit contribute to the variability observed among the audits, which in turn 
influences the confidence intervals presented in this report. If households are not selected 
randomly (which is often the case for practical reasons), there could also be a systematic error if 
there is a bias in the selection of households. This uncertainty has not been accounted for in this 
report and – should it exist – would be very difficult to quantify. The same sampling issues are 
also applicable to Household Waste Recycling Centres (HWRCs) and contamination of dry 
recycling by food waste. In both cases, the random, sampling errors have been estimated and 
reported. 
Having data from only a sample of local authorities 
Random uncertainties  
Having data from a sample of local authorities (rather than the entire population) introduces 
random sampling error into the calculations. The degree of sampling error has been quantified in 
this study using confidence intervals, which take account of both the genuine variation in waste 
composition between local authorities and the additional variation caused by sampling 
households within local authorities (see previous uncertainty). 
Representativeness of local authorities covered by compositional analysis 
Systematic uncertainties  
There are many factors that may influence whether a local authority performs a compositional 
analysis. Analysis of the data and findings in previous Local authority collected household food 
waste in the UK work suggests that local authorities who collect food waste separately had 
PAGE 78
Methods used for Household Food and Drink Waste in the UK 2022 
  
78 
 
lower food waste yields in the residual stream. This has been adjusted for in the calculations 
by stratifying the sample between those collecting food waste in targeted collection and those 
not. 
Total quantity of waste in relevant streams (reported in WasteDataFlow) 
Systematic and random uncertainties  
Information reported to WasteDataFlow undergoes many checks before it is made public. 
However, it is possible that measurement, classification and reporting errors do occur, which 
would feed through to the results in this report. It is not possible to quantify the magnitude of 
these potential errors. 
Estimate of food waste in mixed organics collections 
Systematic uncertainty  
The proportion of food waste in mixed organics collections is highly variable and depends on a 
range of factors including how the collection is communicated to households and the length of 
time since its introduction. This data is only available for a few authorities so it was necessary to 
make a generic assumption for the other authorities with such collections, which could have 
introduced a systematic error into the results. To test the sensitivity of the result to the yield of 
food waste assumed to be in mixed organics collections, yields were doubled and the results 
compared. The influence of this (relatively extreme) change was 2.6% for estimate of local-
authority-collected waste in the UK. 
Estimate of food waste in separate food waste collections 
Systematic uncertainty  
The analysis assumed that 100% of material in separate food waste collections is food or drink 
waste. Data from eleven studies suggests that contamination is low (average of 1.8%). Given this, 
the assumption made in the report is likely to result in an over-estimation of food waste of about 
13,347 tonnes (0.3% of the total UK food waste tonnage). Additionally, a proportion of separate 
food waste collections are made up of food waste liners which are compostable and accepted by 
local authorities, but are not food waste. Of a separate food waste collection about 2.4% are 
liners, this equates to about 18,377 tonnes of lines in the UK per year. Subsequently the separate 
food waste estimate including liners means that the total UK food waste estimate is likely an 
overestimate by about 0.4% as result of liners being included in the reported tonnages.

9.2 Uncertainties associated with detailed waste

Sampling of households 
Random and systematic uncertainties  
It is not practically feasible to perform compositional analysis on all households in the UK, so a 
sample was drawn, leading to random sampling errors which are accounted for at the nation level 
in the confidence intervals quoted in this report. Sampling can lead to systematic errors if the 
sample of households is not representative of the population. Weighting was therefore applied to 
account for over- and underrepresentation of particular categories of household. However, some 
systematic uncertainty may remain from participation bias – those households agreeing to take 
part in the study may differ, with regard to food waste, to those that do not agree.  
Measurement of waste 
Random and systematic uncertainties  
There is the possibility that uncertainties were introduced during the analysis phase. Calibrated 
scales were used to weigh items with a precision of 1 gramme and checks were carried out to 
ensure that the data was entered correctly in the electronic databases. Furthermore, unusual 
coding and weights were investigated during the analysis phase and any anomalies corrected. 
Therefore, it is likely that the risk of substantial uncertainty from this source is small. 
Sampling from England and Wales only 
Systematic uncertainty  
The households sampled were in England or Wales but chosen to be representative of UK 
households on a number of variables. However, if there are systematic differences in the types of 
food and drink waste generated between England and Wales and the rest of the UK, this would 
bias the results. Previous research has indicated that differences in the total amount of waste are 
similar between nations, but little is known about differences in the type of waste.

Random and systematic uncertainties

9.5 Confidence intervals 
Calculation of confidence intervals 
Confidence intervals have been calculated for key estimates in the report. This gives an indication 
of how precise the estimates are. The confidence intervals constructed only measure the effect of 
sampling errors. They do not include sources of systematic uncertainties as discussed in sections 
9.2 and 9.3. In some cases, systematic errors have been explored; in the case of seasonal 
variation in food waste, adjustment has been made to account for it. However, for all other 
systematic errors the uncertainties have not been incorporated into the total uncertainty 
estimates. For this reason, confidence intervals stated are underestimates and represent a lower 
bound on the amount of uncertainty. Therefore, they are referred to as approximate confidence 
intervals.  
 
For each of the primary data sources used in the report, approximate 95% confidence intervals 
were calculated. For the Local authority collected household food waste in the UK 2022 
research, the variability between authorities was used to obtain the confidence intervals. For the 
detailed waste compositional analysis and diary-based research, the variability in the amount of 
waste between households was used. Although the distribution of the amount of waste from 
individual households is non-normal – the distribution is skewed with a right-hand tail – the error 
structure around the mean was assumed normal, invoking the central limit theorem. This 
approach should be valid given the number of households in each sample group (>30). The 
amount of UK household food and drink waste generated in 2021 was 6.0million tonnes ± 0.28 
million tonnes (Table 17).
